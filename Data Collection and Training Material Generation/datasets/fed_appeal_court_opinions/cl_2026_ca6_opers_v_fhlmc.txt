       HELENE N. WHITE, Circuit Judge. The Ohio Public Employees Retirement System
(“OPERS”) brought securities-fraud claims against the Federal Home Loan Mortgage
Corporation (“Freddie Mac”) and three of its senior officers on behalf of itself and all purchasers
of Freddie Mac’s common stock from August 1, 2006 through and including November 20, 2007
(the “Class Period”). In a prior appeal, we reversed the district court’s dismissal of OPERS’
complaint. On remand, the district court denied OPERS’ motion for class certification and
granted summary judgment to all defendants. We REVERSE IN PART, VACATE IN PART,
and REMAND for further proceedings.

                                                I.

                                                A.

       The Ohio Public Employees Retirement System is a state pension fund serving more than
1.3 million Ohio public employees and more than 3,700 public employers. According to the
complaint, OPERS is the 14th largest retirement system in the United States.

       The Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation is a federally chartered corporation
established to support liquidity and stability in the secondary mortgage market. By statute,
Freddie Mac’s business is limited to the purchase and sale of home mortgages and mortgage-
related securities. See 12 U.S.C. § 1454(a)(1). Freddie Mac holds mortgage loans and securities
for investment purposes. During the Class Period, Freddie Mac “purchased loans, securitized
them, and guaranteed the payment of principal and interest on these mortgage-related securities.”
 No. 25-3765                             OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                                         Page 3


Freddie Mac’s Brief at 8. The individual defendants were officers of Freddie Mac during the
Class Period, including Richard F. Syron, the former Chairman of the Board and Chief Executive
Officer, Anthony S. Piszel, the former Executive Vice President and Chief Financial Officer, and
Eugene M. McQuade, the former President and Chief Operating Officer (collectively “individual
defendants”).1

          To finance the purchase of mortgages and mortgage-related securities, Freddie Mac
issues debt and stock offerings. Freddie Mac maintains a Credit Guarantee Portfolio and an
Investment Portfolio. The Credit Guarantee Portfolio contains the Single-Family Guarantee
Portfolio, “which guarantees the payment of principal and interest on residential mortgages that
it purchases in the secondary market.” Ohio Pub. Emps. Ret. Sys. v. Fed. Home Loan Mortg.
Corp., 830 F.3d 376, 380 (6th Cir. 2016) (“OPERS I”). Freddie Mac securitizes these mortgages
as mortgage-backed securities. Id. The Credit Guarantee Portfolio also contains a much smaller
multifamily segment. The Investment Portfolio is Freddie Mac’s “Retained Portfolio,” which
holds mortgage loans and mortgage-related securities for purposes of investment. Freddie Mac’s
Brief at 8.

          Freddie Mac “monitors loan quality through its proprietary automated underwriting
system Loan Prospector.” OPERS I, 830 F.3d at 380. Loan Prospector assigns loans one of six
grades:       The highest grades, A+, A1, A2, and A3, are “Accept Loans,” which can be
automatically underwritten; the remaining grades, C1 and C2, are “Caution Loans,” which carry
higher risk and require manual underwriting. Id.

                                                  II.

        We first address the district court’s order denying class certification. OPERS brought
claims under Sections 10(b) and 20(a) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, as amended, 15
U.S.C. §§ 78j(b), 78t(a), and SEC Rule 10b-5, 17 C.F.R. § 240.10b-5. Under Section 10(b) and
Rule 10b-5, a securities-fraud suit has six elements: “(1) a material misrepresentation or
omission by the defendant; (2) scienter; (3) a connection between the misrepresentation or
omission and the purchase or sale of a security; (4) reliance upon the misrepresentation or
omission; (5) economic loss; and (6) loss causation.” In re Omnicare, Inc. Sec. Litig., 769 F.3d
455, 469 (6th Cir. 2014) (“Omnicare II”) (quoting Matrixx Initiatives, Inc. v. Siracusano, 563
U.S. 27, 37-38 (2011)).

        OPERS sought class certification under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23(b)(3), which
requires a court to find “that the questions of law or fact common to class members predominate
over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). We review a
district court’s order rejecting class certification for abuse of discretion. Reeb v. Ohio Dep’t of
Rehab. & Corr., 435 F.3d 639, 643 (6th Cir. 2006). “The district court maintains substantial
discretion in determining whether to certify a class, as it possesses the inherent power to manage
and control its own pending litigation. However, we will find an abuse of discretion when the
district court relies on erroneous findings of fact, applies the wrong legal standard, misapplies the
correct legal standard when reaching a conclusion, or makes a clear error of judgment.” Id. at
643-44 (citation modified).

        In its class-certification order, the district court determined that OPERS could not
establish reliance in a manner common to the class and that it had not demonstrated that damages
were measurable on a class-wide basis. Class Cert. Order, 2018 WL 3861840, at *20. Because
the district court relied on a faulty premise, we vacate its denial of class certification.

                                                         A.

         “Most plaintiffs in Rule 23(b)(3) securities-fraud class actions hope to establish a
presumption of reliance, which makes it easier to establish predominance by ‘prov[ing] reliance
through evidence common to the class.’” In re FirstEnergy Corp. Sec. Litig., 149 F.4th 587, 602
(6th Cir. 2025) (quoting Goldman Sachs Grp., Inc. v. Ark. Tchr. Ret. Sys., 594 U.S. 113, 119
(2021)). In a misrepresentation case like this one, a plaintiff seeking a presumption of reliance
“must establish: ‘(1) that the alleged misrepresentations were publicly known, (2) that they were
material, (3) that the stock traded in an efficient market, and (4) that the plaintiff traded the stock
between the time the misrepresentations were made and when the truth was revealed.’” Id.
(quoting Halliburton Co. v. Erica P. John Fund, Inc., 573 U.S. 258, 268 (2014)). In its class-
certification order, the district court addressed only number three, concluding that OPERS had
not demonstrated that Freddie Mac’s stock traded in an efficient market. We disagree and
conclude that Freddie Mac traded in an efficient market.2 We also disagree with portions of the
district court’s analysis of whether Freddie Mac has rebutted the presumption of reliance and so
remand for reconsideration of that issue.

         OPERS seeks to establish market efficiency through the expert reports and testimony of
finance professor Dr. Steven P. Feinstein. Dr. Feinstein’s report concludes that the market for
Freddie Mac’s common stock was efficient during the class period. If correct, the efficiency of
the market would create a presumption of reliance, given that “[a]n investor who buys or sells
stock at the price set by the market does so in reliance on the integrity of that price.” Basic Inc.
v. Levinson, 485 U.S. 224, 247 (1988).

         The district court rejected OPERS’ motion for class certification, concluding that
(1) OPERS could not establish reliance because it could not establish market efficiency; (2) even
if OPERS could establish market efficiency and create a presumption of reliance, Freddie Mac
could rebut that presumption; and (3) OPERS failed to demonstrate that common issues of law

         We first examine whether OPERS has demonstrated market efficiency, which would
create a presumption that it relied on any material misrepresentations.3 We have identified the
following five factors as probative of market efficiency: “(1) a large weekly trading volume;
(2) the existence of a significant number of reports by securities analysts; (3) the existence of
market makers and arbitrageurs in the security; (4) the eligibility of the company to file an S-3
Registration Statement; and (5) a history of immediate movement of the stock price caused by
unexpected corporate events or financial releases.” Freeman v. Laventhol & Horwath, 915 F.2d
193, 199 (6th Cir. 1990) (citing Cammer v. Bloom, 711 F. Supp. 1264, 1286-87 (D.N.J. 1989)).
The parties refer to these as the Cammer factors. The parties also point to additional structural
factors probative of an efficient market. Three such factors are set out in Krogman v. Sterritt,
202 F.R.D. 467, 474 (N.D. Tex. 2001), namely “(1) the capitalization of the company; (2) the
bid-ask spread of the stock; and (3) the percentage of stock not held by insiders.” Waggoner v.
Barclays PLC, 875 F.3d 79, 95 (2d Cir. 2017) (quoting Krogman, 202 F.R.D. at 474). One final

         The parties agree that the first three Cammer factors are met.4 Class Cert. Order, 2018
WL 3861840, at *16. The parties also agree that the Krogman factors are met and that Freddie
Mac was traded on the New York Stock Exchange. Id. The parties dispute the fifth Cammer

that Dr. Feinstein did not sufficiently establish it. Id. at *17. The district court therefore
concluded that OPERS failed to establish market efficiency and could not establish reliance.

         We disagree and conclude that OPERS carried its burden to establish market efficiency.
The district court’s conclusion to the contrary rested on its improper elevation of the fifth
Cammer factor and its related misunderstanding and rejection of OPERS’ price-maintenance
theory. Reeb, 435 F.3d at 644 (abuse of discretion occurs when the district court applies the
wrong legal standard).

         The district court concluded that Dr. Feinstein failed to demonstrate market efficiency
because he relied on a single-date event study—the last day of the class period—to assess market
efficiency for the entire Class Period. Class Cert. Order, 2018 WL 3861840, at *2. Event
studies are “regression analyses that seek to show that the market price of the defendant’s stock
tends to respond to pertinent publicly reported events.” Halliburton, 573 U.S. at 280. As the
district court accurately observed, event studies typically demonstrate market reaction on far
more days than the single day chosen by Dr. Feinstein. See, e.g., George v. China Auto. Sys.,
Inc., No. 11-cv-7533, 2013 WL 3357170, at *12 (S.D.N.Y. July 3, 2013); In re Fed. Home Loan
Mortg. Corp. (Freddie Mac) Sec. Litig., 281 F.R.D. 174, 180 (S.D.N.Y. 2012). The district court
therefore concluded that Dr. Feinstein’s event study was unreliable.

         The district court’s analysis suffers from several fatal flaws.    Most significantly, it
rejected OPERS’ price-maintenance theory.            In OPERS I, we expressly recognized “the
alternative theory of materialization of the risk.” 830 F.3d at 384-85 (citing In re Omnicom Grp.,
Inc. Sec. Litig., 597 F.3d 501, 511 (2d Cir. 2010)). As the Second Circuit explained, under the
materialization-of-the-risk theory, a plaintiff demonstrates loss causation by showing “that the
loss was foreseeable and caused by the materialization of the risk concealed by the fraudulent
statement.” In re Omnicon Grp., Inc. Sec. Litig., 597 F.3d at 513 (quoting ATSI Commc’ns, Inc.
v. Shaar Fund, Ltd., 493 F.3d 87, 107 (2d Cir. 2007)). A plaintiff can point to a corrective
disclosure of the fraud or a “‘negative investor inference[]’ drawn from a particular event or
disclosure.” OPERS I, 830 F.3d at 384-85 (quoting In re Omnicon Grp., Inc. Sec. Litig., 597
F.3d at 511).

         The price-maintenance theory is the corollary of the materialization-of-the-risk theory,
applied to demonstrate price impact rather than loss causation. Price-maintenance theory, as the
Second Circuit explained, “recognizes ‘that statements that merely maintain inflation already
extant in a company’s stock price, but do not add to that inflation, nonetheless affect a
company’s stock price.’” Waggoner, 875 F.3d at 104 (quoting In re Vivendi, 838 F.3d 223, 256
(2nd Cir. 2016)). It thus describes the price impact of misrepresentations in a case where the
plaintiff has alleged loss causation on a materialization-of-the-risk theory—although the
misrepresentations do not cause further inflation, they do cause the stock to maintain an inflated
price.

         We therefore reject the district court’s conclusion that OPERS cannot argue that
“misrepresentations artificially maintained the price of the stock until risks materialized.” Class
Cert. Order, 2018 WL 3861840, at *18. The district court should have credited OPERS’ theory
under which defendants’ misstatements did not cause the price of Freddie Mac’s stock to
increase, but rather maintained its price at an artificially inflated level compared with the value
of the stock in absence of the misrepresentations.

         The district court’s improper rejection of the price-maintenance theory impacted its
market-efficiency analysis. Under the fifth Cammer factor, the district court required OPERS to
demonstrate a history of immediate price impact based on news. It is doubtful that a showing on
 No. 25-3765                         OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                             Page 11


the fifth Cammer factor is always required, especially “in cases involving the common stock of
large financial institutions, traded frequently on a national exchange.” Waggoner, 875 F.3d at 98
(finding that a plaintiff need not make a showing on the fifth Cammer factor to demonstrate
market efficiency and invoke the Basic presumption); see also Dougherty v. Esperion
Therapeutics, Inc., No. 16-10089, 2020 WL 6793326, at *4 (E.D. Mich. Nov. 19, 2020)
(explaining that while “several courts have found the fifth Cammer factor to provide the best
evidence of efficiency, most do not treat it as strictly necessary in making the market efficiency
determination”). And of relevance here, we have suggested that a security traded on the New
York Stock Exchange is likely to be in an efficient market. Freeman, 915 F.2d at 199.

       Requiring a strong showing on the fifth Cammer factor is particularly problematic here.
Relying on the price-maintenance theory, OPERS asserts that the fraudulent statements
maintained an elevated price—not that the statements caused the price to increase. It is therefore
unsurprising that Dr. Feinstein struggled to find days on which the market responded to
statements made by defendants because under OPERS’ theory, the statements maintained
Freddie Mac’s stock price rather than increased it. Relatedly, Freddie Mac’s expert found the
absence of efficiency because “the alleged misstatements in the case at bar did not impact
Freddie Mac’s stock price.” Class Cert. Order, 2018 WL 3861840, at *18; see also R. 383-2,
PageID 18904 (“Assuming for the sake of argument that Freddie Mac’s stock traded in an
efficient market, the Company’s stock price would be expected to react quickly and logically to
an alleged misrepresentation if it was material.”). However, the fact that Freddie Mac’s stock
price did not react to the alleged misrepresentations is consistent with OPERS’ theory of price
maintenance—specifically, that the misrepresentations at issue artificially maintained Freddie
Mac’s stock at a consistent inflated price.

       In some ways, OPERS’ price-maintenance theory has more in common with an
omissions-based theory of securities fraud than a misrepresentations-based theory. “In cases
‘involving primarily a failure to disclose,’ the Affiliated Ute presumption establishes a
presumption of reliance if an alleged wrongdoer had an ‘obligation to disclose’ and ‘the facts
withheld [are] material in the sense that a reasonable investor might have considered them
important in the making of [a] decision [to invest].’” In re FirstEnergy Corp. Sec. Litig., 149
 No. 25-3765                       OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                               Page 12


F.4th at 602 (quoting Affiliated Ute Citizens of Utah v. United States, 406 U.S. 128, 153-54
(1972)). Operating under a pure omissions-based theory, a securities-fraud plaintiff need not
establish market efficiency to create a presumption of reliance.      That relaxed standard for
demonstrating reliance is appropriate because of the “unique problem . . . of showing reliance on
something that was never said.” Id. at 603. “Affiliated Ute applies to cases purely or primarily
based on omissions.” Id. at 606. Here, although OPERS’ case is not primarily based on
omissions, the price-maintenance theory implicates similar concerns—the difficulty of proving
price impact (and therefore reliance) when the purported fraud maintains an elevated stock price.
Accounting for this concern, we conclude that the structural evidence of efficiency is sufficient
in this case, particularly given that OPERS made some showing that the market responded as
expected when the risk materialized.

       Our conclusion that Freddie Mac traded in an efficient market is hardly surprising given
its size and the national attention it drew at the time. Freddie Mac’s stock traded at a high
volume on the New York Stock Exchange—Dr. Feinstein estimated that four million shares
changed hands daily. Freddie Mac was followed by both financial firms and news outlets—Dr.
Feinstein’s analysis demonstrated that at least 21 financial-analyst firms followed Freddie Mac
and thousands of news articles were published on Freddie Mac during the class period. Freddie
Mac had numerous market makers—financial intermediaries trading a particular security.
Although not eligible for S-3 registration, Freddie Mac had many of the qualities that make a
security eligible for S-3 registration. Freddie Mac had extremely high market capitalization,
unsurprising given its size. It had a high level of float—the number of shares outstanding minus
the shares held by insiders and affiliates. A stock with high float has a large number of shares
outstanding that are available for trading by investors in the open market, and is thus likely to
attract greater analyst and news media attention—characteristics indicative of market efficiency.
Finally, it had a narrow bid-ask spread—the difference in price between market makers buying
and selling the security—indicating active trading and responsiveness to public information
about the security. These structural factors support our conclusion that Freddie Mac’s stock
traded in an efficient market.
 No. 25-3765                        OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                                Page 13


                                                 2.

       When a plaintiff invokes a presumption of reliance based on market efficiency, “a
defendant [can] rebut this presumption in a number of ways, including by showing that the
alleged misrepresentation did not actually affect the stock’s price—that is, that the
misrepresentation had no price impact.” Halliburton, 573 U.S. at 263-64 (citation modified).
The district court found that Freddie Mac expert “Dr. Bajaj’s report demonstrates that the alleged
misstatements in the case at bar did not impact Freddie Mac’s stock price, rebutting the
presumption of reliance.” Class Cert. Order, 2018 WL 3861840, at *18. But this analysis
repeats the same mistake that the district court made in its rejection of OPERS’ price-
maintenance theory. The fact that Freddie Mac’s stock price did not change in response to the
alleged misrepresentations does not rebut the presumption of reliance in a case premised on
price-maintenance theory. Indeed, one would expect to see no price change in response to
misrepresentations and omissions designed to hide an emerging risk.

       The district court also cited Dr. Bajaj’s conclusion that the disclosures on November 20,
2007 were not linked to the alleged misrepresentations and omissions. Id. at *18. If true, that
would “sever[] the link between the alleged misrepresentation and . . . the price” of Freddie
Mac’s stock, effectively rebutting the Basic presumption of reliance. Halliburton, 573 U.S. at
269. But the district court did not clearly adopt Dr. Bajaj’s finding on this point. Further,
because we reject other elements of the district court’s analysis of Freddie Mac’s attempted
rebuttal of the presumption of reliance, it is not clear whether this portion of Dr. Bajaj’s report,
alone, demonstrates lack of price impact by a preponderance of the evidence. In re Acadia
Healthcare Co., No. 22-0506, 2023 WL 3620955, at *2 (6th Cir. May 23, 2023) (when the
plaintiff moves for class certification and establishes the Basic presumption, “the defendant bears
the burden of persuasion to prove a lack of price impact, which it must show by a preponderance
of the evidence”) (citation modified); Arkansas Tchrs. Ret. Sys. v. Goldman Sachs Grp., Inc., 879
F.3d 474, 485 (2d Cir. 2018). We therefore vacate the district court’s conclusion that Freddie
Mac rebutted the presumption of reliance and remand for reconsideration of the evidence in light
of OPERS’ valid theory of price maintenance.
 No. 25-3765                        OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                              Page 14


                                                3.

       In addition to concluding that OPERS failed to establish reliance through evidence of
market efficiency, the district court concluded that OPERS “has not adequately demonstrated
that common issues of law and fact will predominate over individual issues with respect to the
reliance element of its Exchange Act claims.” Class Cert. Order, 2018 WL 3861840, at *20.
But given the district court’s conclusion that OPERS could not establish reliance, it would be
impossible for OPERS to demonstrate that common issues of fact and law predominate in its
proof of reliance. Consequently, we vacate and remand for reconsideration of commonality in
light of our price-maintenance analysis.

                                               B.

       The district court also determined that OPERS failed “to establish that damages can be
measured on a class-wide basis in a manner consistent with its theories of liability.” Class Cert.
Order, 2018 WL 3861840, at *20. “Class certification under Rule 23(b)(3) requires that a
plaintiff establish ‘that damages are capable of measurement on a classwide basis.’” FirstEnergy
Corp. Sec. Litig., 149 F.4th at 620 (quoting Comcast Corp. v. Behrend, 569 U.S. 27, 34 (2013)).
Here, Dr. Feinstein testified only in general terms that damages could be calculated on a
classwide basis but did not testify to the specifics of which model he would use. For example, he
stated, “valuation tools, which would include event study analysis such as that described herein,
and potentially other empirical analyses if necessary, would be used to establish that the
disclosure(s), correcting the alleged misrepresentations and omissions, caused the price of
Freddie Mac common stock to fall.” R. 372-3, PageID 18555. To properly calculate damages,
OPERS will have to differentiate the portion of the decrease in stock price attributable to
defendants’ misrepresentations and the portion of the decrease attributable to other market
factors. Dr. Feinstein’s general reference to an event study and “other empirical analyses” is
insufficient. Id. Without providing a more specific proposal, neither Freddie Mac nor the court
can understand the feasibility of measuring damages on a classwide basis.

       Given OPERS’ failure to identify a more specific calculation methodology, the district
court did not abuse its discretion in concluding that OPERS failed to establish that damages
 No. 25-3765                       OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                                Page 15


could be measured on a classwide basis. However, the district court’s rejection of the damages
measurement was bound up in its rejection of OPERS’ price-maintenance theory. And we are
not a court of first review. So on remand, the district court shall permit OPERS to refile its
motion for class certification with a specific proposal for calculating damages under the price-
maintenance theory. See In re ConAgra Foods, Inc., 302 F.R.D. 537, 581 (C.D. Cal. 2014); Fort
Worth Employees’ Ret. Fund v. J.P. Morgan Chase & Co., 301 F.R.D. 116, 144 (S.D.N.Y.
2014); Sicav v. James Jun Wang, No. 12 CIV. 6682 PAE, 2015 WL 268855, at *7 (S.D.N.Y. Jan.
21, 2015).
                                               C.

       Finally, the district court granted Freddie Mac’s motion to exclude the testimony and
reports of Dr. Feinstein. Under Federal Rule of Evidence 702, “the trial judge must ensure that
any and all scientific testimony or evidence admitted is not only relevant, but reliable.” Daubert
v. Merrell Dow Pharms., Inc., 509 U.S. 579, 589 (1993). Daubert affords the district court
“broad latitude” to determine reliability. Decker v. GE Healthcare Inc., 770 F.3d 378, 391 (6th
Cir. 2014) (quoting Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael, 526 U.S. 137, 153 (1999)). “In determining
whether proffered expert testimony meets this standard, district courts generally make three
inquiries: (1) is the witness qualified by knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education?;
(2) is the testimony relevant, meaning that it will assist the trier of fact to understand the
evidence or to determine a fact in issue?; and (3) is the testimony reliable under the factors
outlined in Fed. R. Evid. 702 and the Supreme Court’s decision in Daubert?” Babb v. Maryville
Anesthesiologists P.C., 942 F.3d 308, 316 (6th Cir. 2019) (citation modified). “But ‘rejection of
expert testimony is the exception, rather than the rule.’” In re Scrap Metal Antitrust Litig., 527
F.3d 517, 530 (6th Cir. 2008) (quoting Fed. R. Evid. 702 advisory committee’s note to 2000
amendments) (citation modified).

       The district court’s rejection of Dr. Feinstein’s testimony was bound up in its rejection of
the price-maintenance theory underlying Dr. Feinstein’s testimony, leading the district court to
improperly elevate the fifth Cammer factor and erroneously conclude that the absence of stock
movement in response to the purported misrepresentations indicates no price impact.
In addition, the district court noted that Dr. Feinstein impermissibly offered legal conclusions.
 No. 25-3765                         OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                                 Page 16


But even if a portion of Feinstein’s report relayed legal conclusions, that would not be grounds
for excluding the significant portions that opined on economic facts. See Babb, 942 F.3d at 316
(“[T]he district court used a sledgehammer, when the law required that it use only a scalpel.”).
Finally, the district court pointed to an opinion by the Delaware Court of Chancery that rejected
Dr. Feinstein’s expert opinions.      In that case, the court noted that Dr. Feinstein was “an
experienced academic” but that he had “no real experience in the telecommunications or cable
industries” at issue. Finkelstein v. Liberty Digital, Inc., No. CIV.A. 19598, 2005 WL 1074364,
at *13 (Del. Ch. Apr. 25, 2005). It therefore rejected his valuation analysis of shares of a
company that had survived a merger as a wholly owned subsidiary. Id. But that specialized
subject matter was far afield from this case, which falls well-within Dr. Feinstein’s financial
expertise as a Chartered Financial Analyst, finance professor, and owner of a financial research
firm. And even if that case could cut against Dr. Feinstein’s reliability, it alone is insufficient to
exclude his testimony in full (indeed, the Finkelstein opinion did not disqualify him as an expert;
it simply discounted his opinions). We therefore vacate the district court’s exclusion of Dr.
Feinstein’s testimony and remand for the district court to consider the admissibility of his
testimony in light of our price-maintenance analysis.

                                          *       *       *

        In sum, we vacate the district court’s order denying class certification, reverse its
determination that Freddie Mac’s stock did not trade in an efficient market, vacate its exclusion
of Dr. Feinstein’s testimony, and remand with instructions to allow OPERS another opportunity
to seek class certification.

                                                 III.

        We next review de novo the district court’s order granting summary judgment to
defendants. Bilyeu v. UT-Battelle, LLC, 154 F.4th 396, 402 (6th Cir. 2025). Summary judgment
is appropriate when “there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled
to judgment as a matter of law.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a). On summary judgment, we “draw all
reasonable inferences for the nonmoving party,” but need not “accept mere conjecture and
speculation.” Patterson v. Kent State Univ., 155 F.4th 635, 644 (6th Cir. 2025). Rather, the
 No. 25-3765                         OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                              Page 17


nonmoving party must present “significant probative evidence” placing material facts in doubt.
Walden v. Gen. Elec. Int’l, Inc, 119 F.4th 1049, 1057 (6th Cir. 2024) (quoting Green Genie, Inc.
v. City of Detroit, 63 F.4th 521, 526 (6th Cir. 2023)).

       The district court determined that there were no genuine issues of material fact and so
defendants were entitled to summary judgment on OPERS’ substantive securities-fraud claim
under Section 10(b) and OPERS’ claims of control-person liability under Section 20(a). We
disagree with both conclusions.
                                                 A.

       Section 10(b) provides “a right of action to purchasers or sellers of securities injured by
its violation.” Tellabs, Inc. v. Makor Issues & Rts., Ltd., 551 U.S. 308, 318 (2007). By its terms,
Section 10(b) prohibits any person from using “any manipulative or deceptive device or
contrivance in contravention of such rules and regulations as the [SEC] may prescribe as
necessary or appropriate in the public interest or for the protection of investors.” 15 U.S.C.
§ 78j(b). Promulgated under Section 10(b), Rule 10b-5 prohibits the making of “any untrue
statement of a material fact” or the omission of “a material fact necessary in order to make the
statements made, in the light of the circumstances under which they were made, not misleading.”
17 C.F.R. § 240.10b–5(b). A Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5 securities-fraud claim has six
elements: “(1) a material misrepresentation or omission by the defendant; (2) scienter; (3) a
connection between the misrepresentation or omission and the purchase or sale of a security;
(4) reliance upon the misrepresentation or omission; (5) economic loss; and (6) loss causation.”
Omnicare II, 769 F.3d at 469 (quoting Matrixx, 563 U.S. at 37-38).

       The district court determined that OPERS failed to demonstrate (1) that the defendants
made materially false or misleading statements, (2) scienter, (3) reliance, (4) loss causation, or
(5) a proposed damages calculation and so it granted summary judgment to defendants. That
grant of summary judgment was erroneous.

                                                 1.

       First, taking the evidence in the light most favorable to OPERS, a reasonable juror could
conclude that defendants made materially false or misleading statements. A false statement is
 No. 25-3765                           OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                           Page 18


actionable if the plaintiff can show that “the statement concerned a material fact and that it was
objectively false or misleading” or, where the misrepresentation concerns “soft information” like
predictions and matters of opinions, if the statement was “made with knowledge of its falsity.”
Omnicare II, 769 F.3d at 470 (quoting Indiana State Dist. Council of Laborers & Hod Carriers
Pension & Welfare Fund v. Omnicare, Inc., 583 F.3d 935, 945-46 (6th Cir. 2009) (“Omnicare
I”)). Omissions are also actionable based on a defendant’s “failure to disclose information when
it had a duty to do so.” Id. at 471.

       False statements and omissions are both subject to Section 10(b)’s materiality standard.
Under this standard, “misrepresented or omitted facts are material only if a reasonable investor
would have viewed the misrepresentation or omission as having significantly altered the total
mix of information made available.” Id. at 472 (citation modified) (quoting In re Sofamor Danek
Grp., Inc., 123 F.3d 394, 400 (6th Cir.1997)).         As the Supreme Court has recognized,
“[d]isclosure, and not paternalistic withholding of accurate information, is the policy chosen and
expressed by Congress.” Basic, 485 U.S. at 234. To that end, the Securities Acts “substitute a
philosophy of full disclosure for the philosophy of caveat emptor and thus . . . achieve a high
standard of business ethics in the securities industry.” Id. (quoting SEC v. Cap. Gains Rsch.
Bureau, Inc., 375 U.S. 180, 186 (1963)). In context, the materiality requirement “filter[s] out
essentially useless information that a reasonable investor would not consider significant, even as
part of a larger mix of factors to consider in making his investment decision.” Omnicare II, 769
F.3d at 472 (citation modified) (quoting Basic, 485 U.S. at 234).

       OPERS claims that defendants made false statements falling into four categories:
(i) subprime exposure, (ii) Alt-A exposure, (iii) credit risk exposure, and (iv) underwriting
standards.   Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to OPERS and drawing all
reasonable inferences in OPERS’ favor, OPERS has shown that a reasonable juror could
conclude that defendants made materially false statements about Freddie Mac’s subprime and
Alt-A exposure but not its credit risk and underwriting standards.
 No. 25-3765                        OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                               Page 19


                                   a.      Subprime Exposure

       The “primary fraud” alleged in the operative complaint is defendants’ purported “failure
to disclose Freddie Mac’s true subprime exposure.” R. 166, PageID 5571. OPERS claims that
“[t]hroughout the Class Period, Defendants made affirmative misstatements of fact with respect
to Freddie Mac’s exposure to mortgage loan losses.” Id. At summary judgment, OPERS
produced various statements made by or attributed to defendants minimizing the subprime
exposure of Freddie Mac’s portfolio. On appeal, OPERS focuses on three statements.

       First, it points to a public speech delivered on May 14, 2007, in which CEO Syron stated,
“at the end of 2006, Freddie [Mac] had basically no subprime exposure in our [single-family]
guarantee business.” R. 558-25, PageID 43674. Second, it points to an earnings call on March
23, 2007, in which CFO Piszel stated, “we have little to no exposure to . . . subprime risk layered
mortgage products.” R. 558-24, PageID 34536. Finally, it points to an Information Statement
published by Freddie Mac on August 30, 2007, which characterized subprime loans as those
which “serve borrowers with poor credit payment histories and . . . typically have a mix of
[negative] credit characteristics,” and quantified the subprime exposure at “approximately $2
billion, or 0.1 percent” of the portfolio as of June 30, 2007. R. 559-15, PageID 45096-97.

       OPERS contends that these statements were false. It argues that Freddie Mac knew that
its subprime exposure was significantly higher than it reported.         It points out that Loan
Prospector, Freddie Mac’s proprietary automated underwriting system, classified caution loans
as subprime. R. 559-86, PageID 44106; R. 559-89, PageID 44160; see also R. 559-88, PageID
44151 (email to management indicated that subprime loans “would score Caution in LP”). In
2007, the Monthly Monitoring report indicated that C1 and C2 loans constituted 10.8% of
Freddie Mac’s portfolio. In a similar vein, OPERS notes that Freddie Mac’s “Segmentor” model
predicted the likelihood that a given loan is subprime, and that the model indicated subprime
exposure in Freddie Mac’s 2007 batch of loans was between 9.53% and 11.50% in July 2007.

       Next, OPERS points to an internal email from Freddie Mac’s Head of External Reporting
Doug Levy who warned, before Syron’s speech on May 17, 2007, “[c]ertainly our portfolio
includes loans that under some definitions would be considered subprime. . . . We should
 No. 25-3765                              OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                                        Page 20


reconsider making as sweeping a statement as we have ‘basically no subprime exposure.’” R.
559-102, PageID 40985. OPERS also points to an internal presentation given to the individual
defendants stating that, in February 2007, Freddie Mac “already purchase[d] subprime-like
loans... but with considerably lower fees” and that the “[w]orst 10% of [Freddie Mac’s] Flow
Business” in 2006 were subprime-like loans. R. 559-122, PageID 44550.                              At a Board
presentation in March 2007, the individual defendants were again told Freddie Mac “already
purchase[d] subprime-like loans.” R. 559-101, PageID 44294.

        Freddie Mac contends that it “participated in the subprime market only in the precise
ways it disclosed,” and that its SF Guarantee Portfolio did not purchase subprime loans except a
small amount of previously disclosed and purchased “T-deals.” Freddie Mac’s Br. at 20.7
Freddie Mac cites both internal and external reports stating that, during the Class Period, it had
no subprime loans in its SF portfolio.

        In response to OPERS’ accusations, Freddie Mac argues that “no generally accepted
definition of ‘subprime’ existed.” Id. at 21. For example, Freddie Mac’s annual report in 2006
stated that the company did not categorize the loans in its credit guarantee portfolio as either
prime or subprime, but estimated that it had essentially no subprime exposure “based on lender-
type, underwriting practice and product structure.” R. 548-21, PageID 30351. Levy’s email,
Freddie Mac argues, thus simply indicates Levy’s belief that Syron’s statement was true and his
recommendation that Syron more closely follow the language in Freddie Mac’s disclosure,
which stated that it had no internal definition of “subprime.” Freddie Mac’s Br. at 22. Further,
Freddie Mac argues that if “subprime” refers to a credit score under 620, anyone could determine
Freddie Mac’s exposure to such loans through its public filings. See Kuriakose v. Fed. Home
Loan Mortg. Corp., 897 F. Supp. 2d 168, 182-83 (S.D.N.Y. 2012) (“Freddie Mac disclosed that
6% of its purchases during 2007 were of loans to borrowers with credit scores less than 620, and
11% were of loans to borrowers with credit scores between 620 and 659. These disclosures
formed part of the ‘total mix’ of information available. Through simple arithmetic (11 + 6),



        7The defendants state that T-deals, a subprime-backed structure security, constituted 0.1 or 0.2 percent of
the SF Guarantee Portfolio.
 No. 25-3765                          OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                              Page 21


investors could ascertain that 17% of Freddie Mac’s purchases involved credit scores less than
660.”).

          Finally, Freddie Mac dismisses the statements of its own employees describing its
“Caution Loans” as subprime, stating that they were using the term “pejoratively” or as a
“shorthand.”     Freddie Mac’s Br. at 24.     Freddie Mac argues that these statements do not
demonstrate that its exposure to subprime loans exceeded its disclosure.

          The district court agreed with Freddie Mac. It concluded that Freddie Mac’s internal risk
management systems “served different purposes than the public disclosures.”              Summary
Judgment Order, 2025 WL 2494544, at *3. Therefore, it was reasonable for Freddie Mac to
internally track caution loans and publicly disclaim exposure to subprime loans. The district
court credited Freddie Mac’s suggestion that the authors of internal documents using the terms
“subprime” or “subprime-like” were using those terms “pejoratively,” not to indicate actual
subprime loans. Id.

          Upon review of the record as a whole and taking the facts in OPERS’ favor, OPERS has
provided evidence sufficient to show that there is a genuine dispute of material fact regarding
whether defendants made false or misleading statements about Freddie Mac’s subprime
exposure. Rule 10b-5(b) “requires disclosure of information necessary to ensure that statements
already made are clear and complete.” Macquarie Infrastructure Corp. v. Moab Partners, L. P.,
601 U.S. 257, 264 (2024). In the light most favorable to OPERS, Freddie Mac stated, through its
executives and publicly filed reports, that it had basically no subprime exposure. But the
evidence indicates that Freddie Mac understood that it had far greater exposure to loans that
many would consider subprime because of their risk-layered characteristics—indeed, loans that
its own employees referred to as “subprime” but that were not disclosed as such. Nor were the
statements of Syron and Piszel qualified by reference to a clearly comprehensible definition of
“subprime.”      A reasonable jury could find that Freddie Mac misled investors by publicly
disclaiming any subprime exposure without acknowledging significant exposure to loans that
many would consider subprime. Omnicare, Inc. v. Laborers Dist. Council Constr. Industry
Pension Fund, 575 U.S. 175, 192 (2015) (“[L]iteral accuracy is not enough: An issuer must as
well desist from misleading investors by saying one thing and holding back another”).
 No. 25-3765                         OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                                Page 22


A reasonable juror could find that by disclaiming subprime exposure and not revealing exposure
to Caution loans that investors could reasonably consider subprime, Freddie Mac “omit[ted] to
state a material fact necessary . . . to make [its] statements . . . not misleading.” 17 C.F.R. §
240.10b–5.

       This case resembles the Omnicare securities litigation. In that case, the defendant made
public representations about its compliance with the law despite internal audits revealing fraud.
Omnicare II, 769 F.3d at 462, 477-78. We held that the plaintiff had adequately pleaded material
misrepresentation based on affirmative statements and omissions, noting that the defendant “had
a duty to disclose the results of the audits after issuing its material-compliance statements.” Id.
at 480-81.   Here too, a reasonable juror could determine that Freddie Mac, having made
affirmative representations about its subprime exposure, had a duty to disclose its exposure to
Caution loans, given that investors could reasonably view those loans as subprime.

       This case is also similar to City of Monroe Employees Retirement System v. Bridgestone
Corporation, 399 F.3d 651 (6th Cir. 2005). In that case, a tire manufacturer made various vague
statements of self-praise and confidence about the quality of its tires, which we found to be non-
actionable puffery. Id. at 671. But we came to a different conclusion regarding a public release
stating that the company continually monitored the performance of its tires “and the objective
data clearly reinforces our belief that these are high-quality, safe tires.” Id. at 671-72. This
statement was made in the context of significant safety concerns leading to multiple consumer
lawsuits. Id. at 672. We determined that “[a] reasonable juror could infer that the ‘objective
data’ representation was a direct response to the lawsuits, or to the public challenges to the safety
of Firestone’s tires, or to both.” Id. So too here, a reasonable juror could conclude that Freddie
Mac’s statements disclaiming subprime exposure, without qualification, were likely to mislead
investors who inferred that the statements broadly disclaimed exposure to risky subprime loans.
See id. (“A reasonable juror could also conclude that the statement, without some qualification or
accompanying disclosure of the numerous pieces of evidence that tended to cut the other way,
was a misrepresentation.”).

       Finally, we reject Freddie Mac’s argument that its public disclosures remedy any
misrepresentations.   In OPERS I, we held that the statements relied on by OPERS were
 No. 25-3765                        OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                                Page 23


actionable, despite the regular financial reports that Freddie Mac now says would have allowed
investors to calculate subprime exposure for themselves. That is because “not every mixture
with the true will neutralize the deceptive” and “[i]f it would take a financial analyst to spot the
tension between the one and the other, whatever is misleading will remain materially so, and
liability should follow.” OPERS I, 830 F.3d at 386 (quoting Va. Bankshares, Inc. v. Sandberg,
501 U.S. 1083, 1097 (1991)). Further, even if investors could have calculated the portfolio’s
exposure to low-credit loans, Freddie Mac’s assertions could still be misleading—“a reasonable
and savvy investor might have believed, for instance, that loans to borrowers with low credit
scores were nevertheless not risky because of other mitigating factors.” SEC v. Syron, 934 F.
Supp. 2d 609, 630 (S.D.N.Y. 2013).

       The district court credited Freddie Mac’s assertion that the “Caution loan designation was
an internal risk management tool that classified loans as requiring additional scrutiny, not
necessarily as ‘subprime’ in the traditional sense.”      Summary Judgment Order, 2025 WL
2494544, at *3. But a reasonable juror could conclude that a reasonable investor could have
been misled by Freddie Mac’s assertion that it had no subprime exposure, given that its internal
and undisclosed data suggested significant heightened risk of a similar type.

       Bear in mind that “[d]isclosure, and not paternalistic withholding of accurate information,
is the policy chosen and expressed by Congress.” Basic Inc., 485 U.S. at 234. Section 10(b)
thus demands more fulsome disclosure than the district court required. Freddie Mac’s significant
exposure to loans it knew its investors might consider subprime is not the type of “essentially
useless information that a reasonable investor would not consider significant.” Omnicare II, 769
F.3d at 472 (quoting Basic, 485 U.S. at 234). Rather, it is information that should have been
disclosed “as part of a larger ‘mix’ of factors” that investors would consider in making their
decision. Id. (quoting Basic, 485 U.S. at 234). And on defendants’ motion for summary
judgment, OPERS just has to show that there is a genuine dispute of fact on this issue. It has
done so.
 No. 25-3765                        OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                              Page 24


                                     b.      Alt-A Exposure

       Taking the facts in OPERS’ favor, a reasonable juror could conclude that Freddie Mac
made false representations about its exposure to Alt-A loans. Freddie Mac’s August 30, 2007,
Information Statement states “we have classified mortgage loans as Alt-A if the lender that
delivers them to us has classified the loans as Alt-A, or if the loans had reduced documentation
requirements which indicate that the loan should be classified as Alt-A.” R. 559-15, PageID
45128. Based on that calculation, Freddie Mac classified “approximately $120 billion, or eight
percent” of its SF Guarantee portfolio as Alt-A. Id. OPERS also points to statements by Piszel
and Cook referencing the same estimate.

       OPERS argues that this estimate was false. It points out that Freddie Mac’s Single
Family Sourcing group determined that 29% of loans being purchased were classified as “low/no
doc by the lender,” indicating that they were Alt-A, whereas 18% of loans being purchased were
classified as Alt-A under the external definition. R. 559-104, PageID 44365. Next, it notes that
the internally utilized Mortgage Credit Risk Analytics (“MCRA”) definition revealed 17% Alt-A
exposure across the portfolio. The MCRA definition included loans designated by one of 93
Special Characteristics Codes (“SCC”) by the lenders. The publicly disclosed definition, by
contrast, excluded 65 of the 93 SCCs, resulting in a far lower estimate. Finally, OPERS points to
commentary on the Alt-A definitions from a Freddie Mac internal presentation, which noted the
inconsistency in internal and external definitions of Alt-A, and in particular, the fact that “the
‘External definition’ exclude[s] some of the very largest Alt A programs.” Id., PageID 44367.

       Freddie Mac contends that its estimate of 8% Alt-A exposure was not false based on its
external definition. It points out that the MCRA definition was based on all “low/no doc”
programs identified by lenders. Freddie Mac’s Br. at 35. The MCRA definition, Freddie Mac
argues, included low documentation loans that were not labeled Alt-A or were from borrowers
with high credit quality. Thus, Freddie Mac argues, its external definition was supportable. The
district court agreed, finding that Freddie Mac had justifiably included only loans whose
“reduced documentation requirements indicate that the loan should be classified as Alt-A.”
Summary Judgment Order, 2025 WL 2494544, at *5 (quoting R. 559-15, PageID 45128)
(citation modified). It found that the higher MCRA figure constituted “one internal measurement
 No. 25-3765                        OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                              Page 25


. . . for risk assessment purposes, not necessarily the definitive measure of Alt-A exposure under
Freddie Mac’s disclosed methodology.” Id.

       But a reasonable juror could find that defendants made false representations about
Freddie Mac’s exposure to Alt-A loans. First, the public definition relied on by Freddie Mac
states, “we have classified mortgage loans as Alt-A if the lender that delivers them to us has
classified the loans as Alt-A, or if the loans had reduced documentation requirements which
indicate that the loan should be classified as Alt-A.” R. 559-15, PageID 45128. In applying this
definition, however, Freddie Mac appears to have excluded loans that the lender identified as
Alt-A, but that Freddie Mac itself determined should not be included in the external definition.
In its brief, Freddie Mac justifies this by stating that some of the loans marked Alt-A by the
lender through SCC code were loans of high credit quality. But that is inconsistent with the
published definition, which states that loans are classified as Alt-A if the lender classifies the
loan as Alt-A, not if the lender so classifies them and Freddie Mac agrees. If the published
definition classified loans as Alt-A only if the lender classified the loan as Alt-A and the loan
had reduced documentation requirements indicating it should be classified as Alt-A, perhaps
Freddie Mac’s classification would find support. But the use of “or” indicates that all loans
designated Alt-A by the lender through SCC codes should have been included.

       Second, a reasonable investor could be misled by Freddie Mac’s statements disclosing
8% Alt-A exposure.      As with subprime exposure, given Freddie Mac’s public statements
estimating 8% Alt-A exposure, a reasonable juror could find that Freddie Mac had an obligation
to fully disclose its exposure to loans that a reasonable investor would consider Alt-A. That
conclusion is bolstered by the fact that Freddie Mac’s own employees expressed confusion and
concern that the public representations did not match internal tracking.

                                  c.      Credit Risk Exposure

       We conclude that the remaining statements relied on by OPERS are not actionable.
OPERS argues that Freddie Mac made false representations about its credit risk exposure.
OPERS points to statements made by Freddie Mac’s executives expressing confidence about its
future. Syron stated on June 14, 2007 that Freddie Mac was “much better positioned for long-
 No. 25-3765                           OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                            Page 26


term profitability than we were a year ago.” R. 558-18, PageID 34149. Similarly, Cook stated
on September 10, 2007, that Freddie Mac’s “credit position is relatively strong. . . . Bottom line,
at a time when many of our competitors are weakening, Freddie Mac’s position is growing
stronger.” R. 558-17, PageID 34142.

       The district court concluded that the statements relied on by OPERS “constitute either
accurate historical facts, non-actionable puffery or forward-looking statements protected by the
‘Bespeaks Caution’ doctrine.” Summary Judgment Order, 2025 WL 2494544, at *9. We agree.
As we have explained, “rosy affirmations commonly heard from corporate managers and
numbingly familiar to the marketplace . . . are at best immaterial misstatements.” Newtyn
Partners, LP v. All. Data Sys. Corp., 165 F.4th 947, 961 (6th Cir. 2026) (quoting Omnicare I,
583 F.3d at 944) (citation modified). Corporate leaders are “expected to be confident about their
stewardship and the prospects of the business that they manage.” Id. (quoting Shields v. Citytrust
Bancorp, Inc., 25 F.3d 1124, 1129-30 (2d Cir. 1994)). A reasonable investor would not rely on
those statements. The phrases “better positioned,” “growing stronger,” and “relatively strong”
lack reference to objective standards for investors or the courts to evaluate.

       OPERS characterizes defendants’ statements as “specific comparative assertions” and
statements “anchored in misrepresentation of existing facts.” Reply Br. at 30-31 (quoting In re
Bank of Am. Corp. Sec. Litig., 757 F. Supp. 2d 260, 310 (S.D.N.Y. 2010)) (citation modified).
But we have routinely dismissed claims arising from vague assertions of quality and optimism.
See In re Ford Motor Co. Sec. Litig., Class Action, 381 F.3d 563, 570 (6th Cir. 2004) (no
liability for broad statements about improving quality, prioritizing quality, making customers
safer, and corporate responsibility); Newtyn Partners, LP, 165 F.4th at 964 (“[O]ptimism alone is
not a fair basis for asserting securities fraud.”). The statements OPERS points to are not specific
enough to compel a different result here.

                                  d.      Underwriting Standards

       Finally, we conclude that Freddie Mac’s statements about underwriting standards are not
actionable.   OPERS offers several statements by Syron and Cook extoling Freddie Mac’s
“disciplined approach in underwriting [] credit risk.” R. 558-25, PageID 43673 (Syron speech);
 No. 25-3765                         OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                            Page 27


R. 558-34, PageID 34842 (Syron speech); R. 559-33, PageID 45434-35 (Cook speech); R. 559-
40, PageID 45551 (Cook speech).         It also points to an Information Statement Supplement
published by Freddie Mac in August of 2007 stating, “We take a disciplined approach to the
acquisition of mortgage loans and generally participate in these products when we believe we are
appropriately compensated for the credit risk.” R. 559-15, PageID 45127.

       OPERS contends that these statements were false. It points to a conclusion letter sent by
the Office of Federal Housing Enterprise Oversight, Freddie Mac’s regulator, stating that 80% of
Freddie Mac’s loan volume received exceptions to underwriting standards. R. 559-133, PageID
41497. OPERS also cites an internal report showing that “Untested Mortgage Products”—loans
whose credit risk Freddie Mac could not effectively model—increased from 24% in 2006 to 35%
in June 2007. R. 559-60, PageID 45888.

       At first blush, the statements relied on here appear to be simply more corporate puffery.
But in context, such statements can sometimes be actionable. See Omnicare II, 769 F.3d at 472
(noting that statements that appear to be puffery can be material when viewed in context).
Multiple district courts have held that “where a bank touts but then abandons its conservative
loan underwriting standards without disclosing the change, statements concerning its lending
policies are materially misleading.” Loc. 295/Loc. 851 IBT Emp. Grp. Pension Tr. & Welfare
Fund v. Fifth Third Bancorp., 731 F. Supp. 2d 689, 706 (S.D. Ohio 2010); see also In re
Washington Mut., Inc. Sec., Derivative & ERISA Litig., 259 F.R.D. 490, 505 (W.D. Wash. 2009).
Other district courts have characterized a defendant’s statements describing its underwriting
approach as “disciplined” and “conservative” as “classic examples of puffery.” In re Sec. Cap.
Assur. Ltd. Sec. Litig., 729 F. Supp. 2d 569, 597 (S.D.N.Y. 2010); see also In re JP Morgan
Chase Sec. Litig., 363 F. Supp. 2d 595, 633 (S.D.N.Y. 2005). Here, OPERS has produced
evidence of significant variance from Freddie Mac’s touted underwriting standards, which
supports its contention that the statements are actionable.

       But even assuming that Freddie Mac’s statements that it had a conservative and
disciplined approach could be actionable in certain contexts, Freddie Mac sufficiently disclosed
its changing standards for underwriting. In its 2006 Annual Report, Freddie Mac stated, “[w]e
have been expanding the share of mortgages we purchase that were underwritten by our
 No. 25-3765                               OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                                         Page 28


seller/servicers using alternative automated underwriting systems or agreed-upon underwriting
standards that differ from our system or guidelines.” R. 548-21, PageID 30348. Freddie Mac
argues that this disclosure renders its other statements not misleading. It points out that in a case
involving alleged half-truths, “a statement cannot be misleading when the words spoken and the
facts omitted operate on different ‘levels of generality.’” Newtyn Partners, LP, 165 F.4th at 963
(citation omitted).

         OPERS characterizes Freddie Mac’s statements about its underwriting practices as
“specific representations about a defined, documented practice.” Reply Br. at 23. And, indeed,
had OPERS identified statements by defendants touting Freddie Mac’s underwriting practice in
detail, a broad disclaimer about the increased use of exceptions might be insufficient. But there
is nothing specific about the statements cited in OPERS’ brief—rather, the statements refer in
general terms to a conservative and disciplined approach to underwriting. In contrast, the cases
cited by OPERS in support of its interpretation of Newtyn involved more specific assertions. See
Bridgestone, 399 F.3d at 672 (“[T]he objective data clearly reinforces our belief that these are
high-quality, safe tires.”); Helwig v. Vencor, Inc., 251 F.3d 540, 557 (6th Cir. 2001), abrogated
on other grounds by Tellabs, 551 U.S. at 308 (defendant stated it was “comfortable” with figures
projecting specific earnings).8

         Here, the broad statements about Freddie Mac’s conservative and disciplined approach
were adequately qualified by the general disclosure that its standards were changing. Had
Freddie Mac made more specific representations about its underwriting standards and not
disclosed the frequency of exceptions granted, a different result might be appropriate. But given
the general nature of the statements, a general qualifier sufficed.




         8OPERS also points to FHFA v. Nomura Holding America, Inc., 104 F. Supp. 3d 441 (S.D.N.Y. 2015),
where the Southern District of New York determined that Nomura misleadingly failed to disclose its deviation from

increasingly deviating from underwriting standards.

           In sum, OPERS has adduced sufficient evidence from which a reasonable juror could
conclude that defendants made materially false statements concerning Freddie Mac’s subprime
and Alt-A exposure but not its credit-risk and underwriting standards.

                                                  2.

           We next consider whether OPERS has produced sufficient evidence that defendants acted
with scienter. To show scienter, a plaintiff must establish that the defendant made the false or
misleading representation with scienter, defined as “a mental state embracing intent to deceive,
manipulate, or defraud.”       Tellabs, 551 U.S. at 319 (quoting Ernst & Ernst v. Hochfelder,
425 U.S. 185, 193 n. 12 (1976)). Scienter can be established either by knowing and deliberate
conduct or by reckless conduct. City of Taylor Gen. Emps. Ret. Sys. v. Astec Indus., Inc.,
29 F.4th 802, 812 (6th Cir. 2022). We have defined recklessness in this context as “highly
unreasonable conduct which is an extreme departure from the standards of ordinary care.” Id.
(quoting Doshi v. Gen. Cable Corp., 823 F.3d 1032, 1039 (6th Cir. 2016)). “Recklessness
requires more than negligence and is akin to conscious disregard.” Id. (citation modified).
“Before drawing an inference of recklessness, courts typically require ‘multiple, obvious red
flags.’” Doshi, 823 F.3d at 1039 (quoting PR Diamonds, Inc. v. Chandler, 364 F.3d 671, 686-87
(6th Cir. 2004)). Here, OPERS must show that defendants acted with reckless disregard of their
obligation to disclose information necessary to ensure that their statements were not misleading.

           At summary judgment, we ask whether the plaintiff has adduced sufficient evidence of
scienter. In evaluating scienter, “the court’s job is not to scrutinize each allegation in isolation
but to assess all the allegations holistically.” Tellabs, 551 U.S. at 326.

           We have set out nine non-exhaustive factors that indicate a culpable level of scienter,
though we do not require a plaintiff to demonstrate the presence of all, or even most, of the
factors:

           (1) insider trading at a suspicious time or in an unusual amount; (2) divergence
           between internal reports and external statements on the same subject;
           (3) closeness in time of an allegedly fraudulent statement or omission and the
           later disclosure of inconsistent information; (4) evidence of bribery by a top
 No. 25-3765                        OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                               Page 30


       company official; (5) existence of an ancillary lawsuit charging fraud by a
       company and the company’s quick settlement of that suit; (6) disregard of the
       most current factual information before making statements; (7) disclosure of
       accounting information in such a way that its negative implications could only be
       understood by someone with a high degree of sophistication; (8) the personal
       interest of certain directors in not informing disinterested directors of an
       impending sale of stock; and (9) the self-interested motivation of defendants in
       the form of saving their salaries or jobs.

Helwig, 251 F.3d at 552.

       If a plaintiff adduces evidence to support a finding of scienter, it may still be negated by
the presence of good faith or innocent motivations. See Stavroff v. Meyo, 129 F.3d 1265 (6th
Cir. 1997) (unpublished table decision) (“[A] company’s reliance on the guidance of outside
auditors is inconsistent with the intent to defraud.”); Novak v. Kasaks, 216 F.3d 300, 307 (2d Cir.
2000) (noting that a desire to maintain a high corporate credit rating, corporate profitability, or
stock price, without more, are insufficient motives to establish scienter). OPERS must produce
evidence from which a reasonable juror could find scienter regarding misrepresentations related
to subprime and Alt-A exposure.

       To show scienter for misrepresentations related to subprime exposure, OPERS relies on
Helwig factors two, six, three, and nine. Under factor two, OPERS contends that Freddie Mac’s
internal reports differed from its external statements and those of Syron and Piszel. Relatedly,
under factor six, OPERS points to the contradiction between the statements at issue and recent
internal reports. Also under factor six, OPERS points in particular to the email from Levy
warning against the comments made in Syron’s speech disclaiming subprime exposure. Under
factor three, OPERS points to the fact that key statements occurred within six to ten weeks of the
November 2007 stock collapse. Finally, under factor nine, OPERS contends that the individual
defendants “received over 80% of their compensation from bonuses and stock-related awards
tied directly to portfolio growth and stock price.” Appellant’s Br. at 49. It argues that this
created a personal financial motivation tied to concealing Freddie Mac’s subprime exposure.

       Freddie Mac argues that OPERS’ compensation theory did not appear in the complaint
and is too generic to support an inference of scienter. It also argues that there was no material
distinction between the statements at issue and its internal reports—reiterating the arguments it
 No. 25-3765                              OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                                          Page 31


makes regarding falsity. Further, Freddie Mac argues that there is no evidence Levy’s email was
actually sent to Syron.

         We conclude that OPERS has produced sufficient evidence from which a reasonable
juror could find scienter. Freddie Mac’s internal reports demonstrated significant exposure to
Caution loans and internal reports and comments suggest a recognition that many would consider
the Caution loans to be subprime or subprime-like.9 In fact, the individual defendants each
attended an offsite meeting in February 2007 at which they were warned that Freddie Mac was
“already purchas[ing] subprime-like loans.” R. 559-122, PageID 44550. That was reiterated at a
Board presentation in March 2007 that the individual defendants attended. R. 559-101, PageID
44294. These warnings and the internal reports described above constituted the “multiple,
obvious red flags” that should have alerted the individual defendants that their comments
disclaiming subprime exposure were misleading. PR Diamonds, 364 F.3d at 686-87.10

         OPERS has also adduced sufficient evidence from which a reasonable juror could find
scienter as to Alt-A exposure. Taking the evidence in the light most favorable to OPERS,
defendants disclosed eight percent Alt-A exposure while internally tracking a far higher number.
In fact, OPERS points to internal conversations questioning why the disclosures were narrowing
the definition of Alt-A used for public disclosure. Appellant’s Br. at 33 (citing R. 559-104,
PageID 44367). See Bridgestone, 399 F.3d at 688 (viewing the contents of meetings as “internal
reports”). From these facts, a reasonable juror could find that defendants recklessly disregarded
their disclosure obligations.




         9In a similar vein, Freddie Mac’s “Segmentor” model estimated subprime exposure between 9.53% and
11.50% in July 2007, though it is unclear if Segmentor’s estimates were reported to the individual defendants.
         10The fact that statements by Piszel post-dated some of the relevant internal reports reflecting exposure to

obligation to rectify his statements once he became aware of those documents and that his failure to do so was

          It is true that OPERS relies almost entirely on the divergence between internal reports
and external statements and the disregard of the most current factual information. But “[w]e
have described divergence as the ‘key factor’ to a finding of scienter.” Dougherty v. Esperion
Therapeutics, Inc., 905 F.3d 971, 981 (6th Cir. 2018) (quoting Bridgestone, 399 F.3d at 688). In
Dougherty, we found a strong inference of scienter where the defendant initially stated it would
not need a certain form of regulatory approval but quickly reversed course. Id at 979-80. The
initial statement proved inconsistent with internal meeting notes and the later statement. Id. We
found that discrepancy probative of scienter under three Helwig factors: divergence between
internal and external reports, disregard of recent factual information, and closeness in time
(a factor we found “minimally” weighed in the plaintiff’s favor). Id. at 981. Similarly, in Astec,
29 F.4th at 813, we found scienter as to the defendant Chief Executive Officer based primarily
on the consistent theme of “relentless, unfounded optimism that was contradicted by the
undisclosed facts.” There, the CEO repeatedly reassured investors that a loan would be repaid
despite a clear and undisclosed risk of default. Id. We found that the CEO’s statements of
optimism and non-disclosure of risks he knew about supported a strong inference of scienter.
Id.11 Although these cases were on a motion to dismiss, they still provide guidance regarding the
type of evidence that could be indicative of scienter. And similar discrepancies indicate the
presence of scienter here: defendants disclaimed subprime and Alt-A exposure without
disclosing Freddie Mac’s significant exposure to loans that many would consider subprime and
Alt-A.

          In addition to disputing the factors put forward by OPERS, Freddie Mac and the
individual defendants argue that several Helwig factors negate scienter. See Tellabs, 551 U.S. at
324 (“[A] court must consider plausible, nonculpable explanations for the defendant’s conduct,
as well as inferences favoring the plaintiff.”). Freddie Mac points out that, rather than sell shares
at a suspicious moment (Helwig factor one), Freddie Mac and the individual defendants acquired




          11We acknowledge that the CEO in Astec also sold stock at a suspicious time, a fact not asserted here.   Id.
at 814.
 No. 25-3765                                OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                                            Page 33


additional stocks, and thus lost money. Further, contrary to Helwig factor five, Freddie Mac won
an ancillary lawsuit.12

         The fact that Freddie Mac and the individual defendants ultimately did not profit from
these purported misstatements could convince a jury that defendants did not act with scienter, but
it does not negate scienter as a matter of law. See Tellabs, 551 U.S. at 324 (noting that the
inference of scienter need not be “irrefutable” but must be “compelling . . . in light of other
explanations”). A reasonable jury could conclude that defendants failed to profit from the
scheme because the risk they were concealing materialized, not because of their innocent
motives. More broadly, “the absence of a motive allegation is not fatal” to a securities-fraud
claim. Dougherty, 905 F.3d at 982 (quoting Tellabs, 551 U.S. at 325).

         Nor does Freddie Mac’s “well-developed disclosure process” negate scienter. Freddie
Mac’s Br. at 41. Freddie Mac cites a district court decision from the District of Columbia for the
proposition that an executive’s reliance on accounting and auditing personnel negates scienter.
Id. (citing In re Fed. Nat. Mortg. Ass’n Sec., Derivative, ERISA Litig., 892 F. Supp. 2d 59, 71-74
(D.D.C. 2012)). But this is not a case in which the plaintiff seeks to impute to leadership the
errors of employees preparing public reports.                   Rather, OPERS alleges that Freddie Mac’s
leadership intentionally exploited ambiguities in the definitions of “subprime” and “Alt-A” to
mislead investors. Defendants cannot plead ignorance as to that strategic choice.

                                                           3.

         OPERS must next demonstrate reliance on the misleading statements. The reliance
inquiry ensures “a proper connection between a defendant’s misrepresentation and a plaintiff’s
injury.” Halliburton, 573 U.S. at 267 (quoting Amgen Inc. v. Connecticut Ret. Plans & Tr.
Funds, 568 U.S. 455, 461 (2013)). “The traditional (and most direct) way for a plaintiff to
demonstrate reliance is by showing that he was aware of a company’s statement and engaged in a

         12The Second Circuit affirmed the dismissal of the “ancillary lawsuit” in an unpublished opinion, finding
that the plaintiff in that case failed to meet the pleading requirements for loss causation. Cent. States, Se. & Sw.
Areas Pension Fund v. Fed. Home Loan Mortg. Corp., 543 F. App’x 72, 77 (2d Cir. 2013). Its opinion is of limited
use on the issue of scienter and, in any event, the facts of that case differ significantly from the facts here. The class
period in that case started on the last day of the class period in this case—the purported misrepresentations at issue
here all occurred before the class period in the Second Circuit case.
 No. 25-3765                         OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                               Page 34


relevant transaction based on that specific misrepresentation.” Amgen Inc., 568 U.S. at 461
(citation modified). OPERS acknowledges that it cannot prove individual direct reliance. Reply
Br. at 10.

        But a plaintiff “can in certain circumstances satisfy the reliance element of a Rule 10b–5
action by invoking a rebuttable presumption of reliance.” Halliburton, 573 U.S. at 268. This so-
called fraud-on-the-market theory presumes that “[a]n investor who buys or sells stock at the
price set by the market does so in reliance on the integrity of that price.” Basic, 485 U.S. at 247.
Assuming further that “most publicly available information is reflected in market price, an
investor’s reliance on any public material misrepresentations, therefore, may be presumed for
purposes of a Rule 10b-5 action.” Id.

        OPERS seeks to invoke reliance through the fraud-on-the-market theory. The district
court rejected this theory, referencing its conclusion at the class-certification stage that “OPERS
failed to establish that Freddie Mac’s stock traded in an efficient market during the Relevant
Period.” Summary Judgment Order, 2025 WL 2494544, at *12.

        Freddie Mac argues that, because OPERS failed to directly address the district court’s
summary judgment ruling on reliance in its opening brief, it has waived the argument. “Courts
and litigants often use the terms forfeiture and waiver interchangeably.” Walker v. United States,
134 F.4th 437, 440 (6th Cir. 2025). But here, the fact OPERS failed to explicitly challenge the
district court’s summary judgment ruling on reliance is best analyzed as forfeiture, given the
absence of any “indication that [OPERS] intentionally abandoned [the] argument rather than
merely overlooked it.” Id. at 441.

        We conclude that OPERS did not forfeit its challenge to the district court’s summary
judgment ruling on reliance. In general, “we don’t treat an argument as forfeited if a party,
despite not specifically discussing the issue, raises related or supporting arguments in its
briefing.” Smith v. City of Union, 144 F.4th 867, 878 (6th Cir. 2025) (citation modified). Here,
OPERS has consistently challenged the district court’s conclusion that it failed to demonstrate
market efficiency—the conclusion precluding it from establishing reliance through a fraud-on-
the-market theory.    On appeal, OPERS raised those arguments in the section of its brief
 No. 25-3765                         OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                            Page 35


addressing the district court’s class-certification order rather than the section addressing the
district court’s summary judgment order.        But the core arguments are the same.      So in
challenging the district court’s rejection of its price-maintenance theory, OPERS at least
tangentially challenged its related holding on reliance.

       Moreover, forfeiture in this context is a judge-made doctrine, the primary purpose of
which is to “avoid surprise and prevent ‘sandbagging’” the opposing party. Glennborough
Homeowners Ass’n v. United States Postal Serv., 21 F.4th 410, 415 (6th Cir. 2021) (quoting Bd.
of Regents of Univ. of Wash. v. EPA, 86 F.3d 1214, 1221 (D.C. Cir. 1996)). Here, defendants
were well-aware that reliance is an issue in the case, and there was no nefarious intent on
OPERS’ part. OPERS therefore did not forfeit its challenge to the district court’s summary
judgment ruling on reliance. And because we have rejected the district court’s conclusion that
OPERS failed to establish market efficiency, we likewise conclude that OPERS can establish
reliance through a fraud-on-the-market theory. On remand, Freddie Mac can seek to rebut this
presumption.
                                                 4.

       OPERS must also show loss causation, that is, it must show that the misrepresentations at
issue “caused the loss for which [it] seeks to recover damages.” 15 U.S.C. § 78u-4(b)(4). “[I]n
the securities fraud context, ‘a misstatement or omission is the “proximate cause” of an
investment loss if the risk that caused the loss was within the zone of risk concealed by the
misrepresentations and omissions alleged by a disappointed investor.’” OPERS I, 830 F.3d at
384 (quoting Lentell v. Merrill Lynch & Co., 396 F.3d 161, 173 (2d Cir. 2005)).

       OPERS seeks to establish loss causation under a theory of materialization of the risk.
Under that theory, “a misrepresentation causes a stock price to remain inflated by preventing
preexisting inflation from dissipating from the stock price.” Appellant’s Br. at 13 (quoting
Goldman Sachs, 594 U.S. at 119-20).

       The district court concluded that OPERS failed to establish loss causation as a matter of
law. Summary Judgment Order, 2025 WL 2494544, at *14. But it did so having rejected the
price-maintenance theory, which is a corollary to the materialization-of-the-risk theory. Class
 No. 25-3765                         OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                                Page 36


Cert. Order, 2018 WL 3861840, at *18. In granting summary judgment on the issue, the district
court noted that its denial of class certification could have been altered or amended. Summary
Judgment Order, 2025 WL 2494544, at *14. Thus, it concluded that OPERS should have
produced evidence of loss causation at summary judgment even though its theory underlying that
evidence had been rejected as a matter of law. Finally, the district court pointed out that
OPERS’ theory of loss causation was undercut by the fact that its own expert had predicted a
loss nearly a month before the disclosure OPERS now says caught the market by surprise,
indicating that the market was not, in fact, surprised. Id. at *13. Freddie Mac echoes these
arguments in its briefing.

       Given our acceptance of OPERS’ theory of price maintenance, we also reject the district
court’s conclusion that OPERS failed to establish loss causation as a matter of law. When we
reversed the district court’s prior grant of summary judgment in this case, we stated that, on
remand, “the district court may, but is not required to, revisit the significant issues of law raised
by OPERS.” OPERS II, 64 F.4th at 736.             The parties and the district court appeared to
understand this to be an invitation for the district court to reconsider its rejection of OPERS’
evidence and argument in support of price impact. But the district court did not reconsider its
ruling except to ask, at oral argument on defendants’ summary judgment motions, why OPERS
did not seek reconsideration. OPERS responded that, while it disagreed with the district court’s
price impact ruling, it had no additional evidence or new argumentation. OPERS conceded that
it could not establish damages given the legal rulings in the district court’s class-certification
order and made clear that it intended to appeal those rulings. The district court had rejected
OPERS’ attempt to demonstrate price impact through the price-maintenance theory and, as
counsel for OPERS stated at argument before the district court, “you need price impact for loss
causation for damages. If you don’t have price impact, there are no damages.” R. 594, PageID
42984. The rejection of the price-maintenance theory was thus fatal to OPERS’ loss-causation
theory. Now on appeal, OPERS seeks to do exactly what it said it would—appeal the order
rejecting the legal theory underlying its case for price impact, loss causation, and damages. We
see no reason why OPERS should have presented evidence and argument to the district court
under a theory the district court had already rejected.        On remand, OPERS will have to
demonstrate that the purported misrepresentations at issue caused the losses it suffered.
 No. 25-3765                        OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                               Page 37


We therefore vacate the district court’s holding and remand to allow OPERS to present evidence
of loss causation in light of our price-maintenance analysis.

                                                 5.

       Finally, to succeed on its § 10(b) claims, OPERS must show damages. In its summary
judgment order, the district court concluded that OPERS “is precluded from offering any
damages evidence at trial.” Summary Judgment Order, 2025 WL 2494544, at *16. It did so
because OPERS “failed to comply with its mandatory disclosure obligations regarding damages
computations.” Id. at *15.

       OPERS had an obligation under Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(a)(1)(A)(iii) to produce a damages
computation. And under Rule 37(c), a party is prohibited from using information not disclosed
under Rule 26(a) at trial “unless the failure was substantially justified or is harmless.” Fed. R.
Civ. P. 37(c)(1). This court considers five factors in determining if the failure to disclose
evidence was substantially justified or harmless: “(1) the surprise to the party against whom the
evidence would be offered; (2) the ability of that party to cure the surprise; (3) the extent to
which allowing the evidence would disrupt the trial; (4) the importance of the evidence; and
(5) the nondisclosing party’s explanation for its failure to disclose the evidence.” Howe v. City
of Akron, 801 F.3d 718, 748 (6th Cir. 2015) (quoting Russell v. Absolute Collection Servs., Inc.,
763 F.3d 385, 396-97 (4th Cir. 2014)). We review a district court’s imposition of sanctions for
noncompliance for abuse of discretion. Id. at 747.

       The district court concluded, and Freddie Mac argues, that OPERS’ nondisclosure was
not substantially justified because OPERS had no reason for failing to disclose its computation
and late disclosure would surprise the defendants and delay trial. Summary Judgment Order,
2025 WL 2494544, at *16.        The district court also noted the importance of the damages
calculation to OPERS’ claims. Id.

       As with the issue of loss causation, the district court’s erroneous rejection of OPERS’
underlying theory excuses OPERS’ failure to present evidence of damages at summary
judgment. We discount the concerns raised by Freddie Mac and the district court about unfair
surprise and the risk of delay to trial. OPERS acknowledged to the district court that it could not
 No. 25-3765                               OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                                          Page 38


survive summary judgment given the class-certification order rejecting its theory of price impact.
See R. 594, PageID 42985 (“And on both loss causation and damages, Dr. Tabak clearly opined
that he was not able to calculate -- to do a loss causation analysis or calculate damages consistent
with this Court’s price impact analysis.”).               OPERS has thus been candid throughout this
litigation that it cannot present evidence of damages unless rulings from the district court’s class-
certification order are overturned on appeal. There was no chance for trial to go forward as
scheduled after summary judgment—OPERS conceded that the summary judgment against it
was warranted based on the district court’s earlier ruling—and thus there was no risk that
defendants would be caught by surprise.                  On remand, OPERS must produce a damages
calculation that defendants will have the opportunity to fully interrogate.

                                                 *        *        *

         In sum, we conclude that OPERS can proceed on its § 10(b) claims based on
misrepresentations concerning subprime and Alt-A exposure. On remand, OPERS will have the
opportunity to demonstrate loss causation and damages in a manner consistent with its theory of
fraud.

                                                          B.

         In addition to bringing § 10(b) claims for false or misleading statements against
defendants Syron and Piszel, OPERS brings § 20(a) claims against all the individual defendants

       THAPAR, Circuit Judge, concurring. Securities litigation can be lengthy and complex.
This case is Exhibit A: It’s trudged on for 18 years, delaying closure for all parties involved.
Judges should do their best to accelerate and simplify complicated lawsuits. So in the hope that
this case doesn’t last another 18 years, I write separately to clarify three distinct issues that recur
in complex cases like this one—and to urge all involved to make this case a priority going
forward.

       First, the majority rightly approves OPERS’s price-maintenance theory of securities
fraud. I write to further explain why price maintenance is a valid securities-fraud theory.
Second, the district court made summary judgment more complicated than it needed to be.
Because this case involved a securities class action, the district court demanded that OPERS
produce evidence giving rise to a strong inference of scienter at summary judgment. But that
demand raises the bar higher than the Federal Rules have set it. Third, this litigation has lingered
for far too long, hurting the parties, courts, and taxpayers. Attorneys and judges should use all
the tools at their disposal to facilitate the movement of cases so they don’t drag on.

                                                  I.

       The district court’s erroneous holdings all stemmed from one fatal flaw: its rejection of
OPERS’s price-maintenance theory. As the majority explains, that theory recognizes that a
company can distort its stock price through false statements that keep the price steady when
truthful disclosures would have caused it to fall. It makes sense that plaintiffs can pursue such a
theory, and I write separately to further elaborate on the majority’s thoughtful analysis.

       For starters, the price-maintenance theory is valid as a matter of logic and economics. If
a company’s stock price is already inflated, a misrepresentation can artificially maintain that
inflation by confirming the market’s expectations. In other words, the misrepresentation can
prevent the price from falling by as much as it would have if the company had been truthful.
 No. 25-3765                         OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                                Page 41


So the economic effect of unlawful price maintenance is that a stock’s price is higher than its real
value—no different from a classic securities-fraud violation.

       Consider a hypothetical that everyone would agree violates the securities laws. Suppose
the market expects a company to earn profits of $100. In reality, the company earns only $80,
but the CEO falsely reports profits of $110. The company’s stock price would jump because the
CEO’s report exceeded the market’s expectations, whereas a truthful disclosure would have sunk
the price. There’s no doubt that violates our securities laws.

       How does the price-maintenance theory differ? The setup is the same: The market
expects $100 in profits, while the real profits are $80. But this time, the CEO falsely reports
$100 in profits (not $110). So instead of jumping, the stock price stays the same. But just as
before, the misrepresentation prevents the stock from falling to its true price, as it would have
with a truthful disclosure. And that’s the critical point. When an investor buys a company’s
stock, he relies on the price being a true reflection of the company’s value. So the investor is
harmed by the company’s misrepresentation whenever it has kept the stock’s price above its true
price. The harm doesn’t turn on whether the misrepresentation actively raised the stock’s price
above its original, previous price. It stems from the fact that the misrepresentation raised or kept
the stock’s price above its true value. At the end of the day, the misrepresentation has led to the
investor paying more for the company’s stock than it’s really worth, and that’s what matters.

       To see why, just tweak the hypothetical. Suppose the company’s true profits are still $80,
but the CEO reports $90—falling short of the market’s expectations of $100. The stock price
would fall, but not as far as it should have. That means there’d still be a gap between the stock’s
true price and the price that investors relied on. As Judge Easterbrook put it, the version of the
hypothetical where the stock price rises is “just a mirror image” of the version where it falls, only
“in black ink, rather than red.” Schleicher v. Wendt, 618 F.3d 679, 683 (7th Cir. 2010). Whether
the market price goes up, down, or sideways, investors depend on it being accurate when they
purchase a security. So disclosures that distort the security’s true price may be fraudulent. The
price-maintenance theory is just one application of that straightforward principle.
 No. 25-3765                        OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                               Page 42


       Nevertheless, the district court rejected OPERS’s invocation of the price-maintenance
theory, citing a Southern District of New York case from 2008. Ohio Pub. Emps. Ret. Sys. v.
Fed. Home Loan Mortg. Corp., No. 4:08-CV-160 (BYP), 2018 WL 3861840, at *18 (N.D. Ohio
Aug. 14, 2018) (citing In re Credit Suisse First Bos. Corp. (Lantronix Inc.) Analyst Sec. Litig.,
250 F.R.D. 137, 145 (S.D.N.Y. 2008)). But since 2008, the Second Circuit has repeatedly
approved the price-maintenance theory. See, e.g., Waggoner v. Barclays PLC, 875 F.3d 79, 104
(2d Cir. 2017). The Second Circuit recognized that when a defendant’s misstatement “prevents
[the] preexisting inflation in a stock price from dissipating,” he can’t avoid liability simply
because his misstatement didn’t cause the stock price to rise. In re Vivendi, S.A. Sec. Litig., 838
F.3d 223, 258 (2d Cir. 2016) (alteration in original) (quoting FindWhat Inv. Grp. v.
FindWhat.com, 658 F.3d 1282, 1317 (11th Cir. 2011)).

       The district court’s rejection of the price-maintenance theory also tainted its market-
efficiency analysis. OPERS tried to show that Freddie Mac traded in an efficient market—
meaning one where the stock price rapidly incorporates and reflects all publicly available
information. A market-efficiency analysis involves a multifactor test whereby courts consider
five so-called Cammer factors and four additional structural factors. See Cammer v. Bloom, 711
F. Supp. 1264, 1286–87 (D.N.J. 1989). The fifth Cammer factor considers whether there’s a
history of immediate changes in a stock price following a disclosure. Id. at 1287. As the
majority explains, the district court wrongly elevated that factor above the others, ignoring
commonsense indications that Freddie Mac traded in an efficient market. But there’s another
problem with relying on the fifth Cammer factor. The question of whether the disclosures
immediately moved the stock price is of little relevance in a price-maintenance case. If OPERS
is right, Freddie Mac’s disclosures were fraudulent because they froze its stock price. So, had
the district court recognized the price-maintenance theory’s validity, it should have put little
weight on Cammer factor five. These two incorrect holdings permeated the district court’s entire
analysis.

                                                II.

       The district court also made a subtler legal error. Securities-fraud plaintiffs must prove
scienter—that the defendant knew his statement was false. What must a plaintiff show to survive
 No. 25-3765                         OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                                Page 43


summary judgment on the element of scienter? That question is the subject of a circuit split, and
our court hasn’t squarely weighed in. The district court nevertheless required the plaintiff to
advance evidence justifying a “strong inference” of scienter. Ohio Pub. Emps. Ret. Sys. v. Fed.
Home Loan Mortg. Corp., No. 4:08-CV-160 (BYP), 2025 WL 2494544, at *17 (N.D. Ohio Aug.
29, 2025). But that’s not quite right.

       The problem? Summary judgment is the wrong time to apply the strong-inference
standard.   True, under the Private Securities Litigation Reform Act of 1995 (PSLRA), a
securities-fraud class-action complaint must include allegations raising a “strong inference” of
scienter to survive a motion to dismiss. 15 U.S.C. § 78u–4(b)(2). But “[a]lthough the PSLRA
substantially raised the pleading standard for scienter, it did not change any substantive intent
requirements.” Mizzaro v. Home Depot, Inc., 544 F.3d 1230, 1238 (11th Cir. 2008). So district
courts can’t just import the elevated pleading standard into their review at summary judgment.

       In fact, three of the four circuits that have squarely addressed this issue have held that the
strong-inference standard doesn’t apply at summary judgment. After all, the standards for the
pleading stage and for summary judgment are different. At the pleading stage, a court asks
“what a reasonable person would think” if the plaintiff’s allegations were true. Id. at 1239. But
at summary judgment, the court must ask “what a reasonable person could think” based on the
evidence advanced before trial. Id. In other words, “the court’s task is to determine based on the
record whether there is a genuine issue of material fact requiring trial.” Costello v. Grundon,
651 F.3d 614, 636 (7th Cir. 2011); see also Howard v. Everex Sys., Inc., 228 F.3d 1057, 1064
(9th Cir. 2000). So in setting a pleading standard, “the PSLRA” didn’t “change[] the well-
established summary judgment standard.” Costello, 651 F.3d at 636.

       Only the First Circuit has come out the other way. Geffon v. Micrion Corp., 249 F.3d 29,
36 (1st Cir. 2001). It argued that the “reasoning” behind the “heightened pleading requirements
is at least as forceful” at summary judgment. Id. That’s because the First Circuit envisioned the
pleading stage and the summary-judgment stage as sitting on a continuum with a plaintiff
needing more evidence to survive a defendant’s motion for summary judgment. See id. So the
First Circuit concluded that if a strong inference of scienter is needed at the pleading stage, at
least the same must be required at summary judgment. Id.
 No. 25-3765                        OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                               Page 44


       In my view, the Eleventh, Seventh, and Ninth Circuits have the better argument. We
regularly hold parties to the normal summary-judgment standard even if they have a higher or
lower pleading standard. For example, a pro se complaint faces “less stringent standards” at the
pleading stage. Erickson v. Pardus, 551 U.S. 89, 94 (2007). But pro se litigants must still raise a
genuine issue of material fact to defeat a motion for summary judgment. See, e.g., Reneer v.
Sewell, 975 F.2d 258, 259–60 (6th Cir. 1992). And False Claims Act cases, like securities cases,
have a heightened pleading standard. United States ex rel. Laughlin v. Radiation Therapy Servs.,
P.S.C., 148 F.4th 791, 798–99 (6th Cir. 2025). Yet we still apply the normal summary-judgment
standard in those cases too. Id. at 802. So even if Congress alters a pleading standard, that
doesn’t give us the power to alter the summary-judgment standard.

       Plus, the rationale for changing the pleading standards in the PSLRA doesn’t apply at
summary judgment. Congress passed that act “[a]s a check against abusive litigation by private
parties.” Tellabs, Inc. v. Makor Issues & Rts., Ltd., 551 U.S. 308, 313 (2007). That check was
necessary because plaintiffs can ordinarily survive a motion to dismiss by pleading facts without
providing any evidentiary support. So if plaintiffs simply pepper their complaints with enough
facts to survive a motion to dismiss, they can then saddle defendants with burdensome discovery
requests, which might even coerce a settlement. But summary judgment is different. At that
stage, the nonmoving party must produce evidence sufficient to establish a genuine dispute of
material fact. Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a). Because it requires evidence—not just allegations—the
normal summary-judgment standard is sufficient to guard against abusive or frivolous litigation.
Applying the PSLRA pleading standard at summary judgment would hold plaintiffs to a doubly
high burden, which isn’t required by the statute or common sense. So the district court erred by
imposing that burden on OPERS.

       To be fair, the district court’s misstep was understandable. Our circuit has used the
“strong inference” of scienter language in a summary-judgment case before. See Brown v.
Earthboard Sports USA, Inc., 481 F.3d 901, 917 (6th Cir. 2007). But that language wasn’t a
holding. Instead, it appeared in a stray quote copied and pasted from a motion-to-dismiss case.
Id. (quoting City of Monroe Emps. Ret. Sys. v. Bridgestone Corp., 399 F.3d 651, 683 (6th Cir.
2005)). For a statement to be a binding holding, “it must be clear that the court considered the
 No. 25-3765                              OPERS v. FHLMC, et al.                                         Page 45


issue and consciously reached a conclusion about it.” Wright v. Spaulding, 939 F.3d 695, 702
(6th Cir. 2019). Since Brown didn’t do that, its errant language doesn’t bind us. So going
forward, district courts shouldn’t impose the PSLRA’s heightened pleading standard at summary
judgment.

                                                        III.

        Finally, this case has dragged on for 18 years with no end in sight. Courts and parties
alike must remember that they have a joint obligation “to secure the just, speedy, and
inexpensive determination” of every case. Fed. R. Civ. P. 1. The resolution of this case has been
anything but just, speedy, and inexpensive. That’s unfair to the parties, the court system, and the
American people. Fortunately, there are plenty of tools available to help district courts and
parties to live up to Rule 1’s command.

        Start with the first problem: the costs to the parties. We don’t yet know what the total
attorneys’ fees and costs will be in this case. But past securities cases make clear that the costs
to the parties will be high. For example, one securities case settled after 16 years for $100
million plus $33 million in plaintiffs’ fees. Erica P. John Fund, Inc. v. Halliburton Co., No.
3:02-CV-1152-M (BMGL), 2018 WL 1942227, at *17 (N.D. Tex. Apr. 25, 2018). Defendants’
costs, while not public, are often even higher than plaintiffs’. So at a minimum, we can estimate
Halliburton’s costs to be at least $33 million. Add that all up, and you get a total cost of around
$66 million. OPERS’s suit has already been going on for 18 years, so the cost may similarly
exceed $50 million.

        In addition to the financial burden, the parties have been forced to spend 18 years of their
lives litigating this suit. The plaintiffs are Ohio retirees who still haven’t been able to recover
any of their alleged losses. The individual defendants are now in their 70s and 80s. They’ve
endured nearly two decades with this case hanging over their heads. Civil litigation is stressful
and disruptive, and prolonged litigation has a negative effect on litigants’ mental health. 1 So
judges should be cognizant of the mental-health costs and the human toll of delay. In fact, this

       Litigation delays also burden judges and the federal courts. Recognizing this, Congress

plaintiffs might be discouraged from pursuing meritorious cases if they think they must spend
tens of millions of dollars and wait nearly two decades for a resolution.

       All these concerns raise an important question—why has the resolution of this case been
so slow? This case has involved multiple complaints, repeated extensions, and long periods
while motions have been pending before the district court—up to a year in one instance.

       District courts and litigants have tools at their disposal to prevent cases from dragging on
as long as this one has. For example, judges can set firm discovery deadlines and speedily
resolve any discovery disputes.2 They can issue oral rulings from the bench so that the parties
don’t have to wait for a written opinion—especially when those rulings aren’t reviewable (for
example, a denial of a motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim). And they can set fixed
deadlines for motions while granting only the most necessary extensions. These mechanisms are
just a small sampling of a district judge’s tools to cut down the length of litigation.

       The parties have recourse as well. First, they shouldn’t request repeated extensions.
Second, they should follow up on outstanding motions. A district judge has a responsibility to
dispose of motions that have been pending for six months. Indeed, Congress has recognized the
dangers of undue backlog and sought to hold district courts accountable for motions pending for
longer than six months. See 28 U.S.C. § 476. Congress believed that public shaming would be
enough, but sadly, that hasn’t worked as well as it should have. So if a district judge is sitting on
a motion for longer than six months, the parties have options. They can first request a status

all have an obligation to move cases along, and when “a district court persistently and without
reason refuses to adjudicate a case properly before it, the court of appeals may issue the writ.”
Will v. Calvert Fire Ins. Co., 437 U.S. 655, 661–62 (1978). Indeed, we’ve recognized that

Congress’s six-month clock. Madden v. Myers, 102 F.3d 74, 78 (3d Cir. 1996). After all, the
“sense of Congress” is that cases “should be adjudicated within six months,” which counsels in
favor of mandamus—even if six months isn’t a firm “deadline.” Barrios Garcia v. U.S. Dep’t of
Homeland Sec., 25 F.4th 430, 453–54 (6th Cir. 2022). Congressional policy and common sense
agree: When judges unduly delay, parties should seek mandamus.

        It’s true that mandamus is an extraordinary remedy. And I recognize parties may worry
that a mandamus petition will draw the district court’s ire. So I encourage litigants to cite this
opinion if anyone questions why they are seeking mandamus. We wouldn’t tolerate it if a
litigant took nine months to a year to file a brief. Likewise, we shouldn’t tolerate it if a district
court takes that long to issue a decision.
