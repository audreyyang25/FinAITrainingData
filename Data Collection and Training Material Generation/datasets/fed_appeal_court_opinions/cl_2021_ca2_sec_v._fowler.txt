22         The principal questions presented on appeal are (1) whether 28 U.S.C.

23   § 2462, which imposes a five-year statute of limitations on Securities and

24   Exchange Commission (SEC) enforcement actions for civil penalties, is

25   jurisdictional and not subject to tolling by the parties; (2) whether excessive

26   trading in customer accounts constitutes a violation of customer suitability




                                               2
 1   requirements as well as churning; 1 and (3) whether civil penalties were properly

 2   imposed based on the number of defrauded customers in this case. We hold that

3    the five-year statute of limitations in § 2462 is not jurisdictional and may be

4    tolled by the parties. We also conclude that the SEC’s suitability claim and the

5    civil penalties imposed in this case were proper and that the other challenges on

6    appeal are without merit. After modifying the judgment to correct one error in

7    the amount of disgorgement, we affirm.

14                                      DISCUSSION

15                                               I

16         On appeal, Fowler makes a number of arguments, the most serious of

17   which is that the relevant five-year statute of limitations for SEC enforcement




                                                 9
 1   actions, 28 U.S.C. § 2462, is jurisdictional and could not be tolled by agreement

 2   between the parties. We address that argument first.

 3          The SEC alleged that Fowler’s fraudulent scheme began in 2011, meaning

 4   that the statute of limitations would ordinarily have expired in 2016. To buy

 5   more time, the SEC and Fowler entered into two agreements that together tolled

 6   the statute of limitations for a year, from March 1, 2016 through February 28,

 7   2017. The SEC ultimately sued on January 9, 2017, well within the tolled statute

 8   of limitations period. 5

 9          Fowler maintains that the statute of limitations is jurisdictional and not

10   subject to tolling, so that the limitations period clearly lapsed by 2016 and the

11   SEC could not have sued him thereafter for any of the misconduct alleged in this

12   case. His position runs headlong into the Supreme Court’s decision in

13   Henderson ex rel. Henderson v. Shinseki, 562 U.S. 428 (2011). There the Court

14   held that “[f]iling deadlines . . . are quintessential claim-processing rules” that

15   “should not be described as jurisdictional” absent a “clear indication that



     5A recent amendment to the Exchange Act took the SEC’s claims for disgorgement and

     the claims in this case accrued more than ten years before they were brought. The
     SEC’s claim for civil penalties against Fowler remains subject to § 2462’s five-year

 1   Congress wanted the rule to be jurisdictional.” Id. at 435–36 (quotation marks

 2   omitted). The “bright line rule for deciding such questions” therefore turns on

 3   clear congressional intent. Id. at 435 (quotation marks omitted). In other words,

 4   Fowler “must clear a high bar to establish” that the statute of limitations “is

 5   jurisdictional.” United States v. Kwai Fun Wong, 575 U.S. 402, 409 (2015).

 6   “[T]raditional tools of statutory construction must plainly show that Congress

 7   imbued a procedural bar with jurisdictional consequences.” Id. at 410. Without

 8   “a clear statement, . . . courts should treat [statutes of limitations] as

 9   nonjurisdictional.” Id. at 409 (quotation marks omitted).

10         28 U.S.C. § 2462 provides, in relevant part, that “[e]xcept as otherwise

11   provided by Act of Congress, an action, suit or proceeding for the enforcement of

12   any civil fine, penalty, or forfeiture, pecuniary or otherwise, shall not be

13   entertained unless commenced within five years from the date when the claim

14   first accrued.” Focusing on the phrase “shall not be entertained,” Fowler says

15   that the plain text of the statute supports his argument that it is jurisdictional.

16   See SEC v. Graham, 21 F. Supp. 3d 1300, 1308 (S.D. Fla. 2014) (holding that the

17   phrase “shall not be entertained” in this context “amounts to an unequivocal

18   statutory command to federal courts not to entertain an untimely claim”



                                                11
 1   (quotation marks omitted), aff’d in part on other grounds, rev’d in part on other

 2   grounds, 823 F.3d 1357 (11th. Cir. 2016). As Fowler suggests, the Supreme Court

 3   has described subject matter jurisdiction as “the classes of cases a court may

 4   entertain.” Fort Bend Cnty. v. Davis, 139 S. Ct. 1843, 1848 (2019). But the Court

 5   has also explained that most statutes of limitations are nonjurisdictional “even

 6   when the time limit is important (most are) and even when it is framed in

 7   mandatory terms (again, most are); indeed, that is so however emphatic[ally]

 8   expressed those terms may be.” Wong, 575 U.S. at 410 (emphasis added)

 9   (quotation marks omitted). For that reason, the phrase “shall not be

10   entertained,” on which Fowler so heavily relies, does not itself tell us that

11   Congress intended § 2462 to be jurisdictional.

12         The statutory history of § 2462 signals that Congress did not intend to

13   impose a jurisdictional requirement where it did not previously exist. In 1948

14   Congress changed the statutory language from “[n]o suit or prosecution . . . shall

15   be maintained” if not brought within a five-year period to the current language,

16   which (again) provides that “an [enforcement] action . . . shall not be

17   entertained” if not brought within a five-year period. Compare 28 U.S.C. § 791

18   (1946) (emphasis added), with Pub. L. No. 80-773, § 2462, 62 Stat. 869, 974 (1948)



                                              12
 1   (emphasis added). Recognizing that the predecessor statute was not itself

 2   jurisdictional, Fowler submits that the amendment was designed to give the

 3   statute jurisdictional teeth. But the amendment does no such thing. First of all,

 4   the amendment is “presume[d]” not to “work[] a change in the underlying

 5   substantive law unless an intent to make such a change is clearly expressed.”

 6   John R. Sand & Gravel Co. v. United States, 552 U.S. 130, 136 (2008) (quotation

 7   marks omitted). Replacing the term “maintained” with “entertained” in 1948

 8   does not clearly express an intent to convert § 2462 into a jurisdictional statute.

 9   And the Reviser’s Notes to § 2462 contained in the House Committee report

10   confirm that the “[c]hanges were made in phraseology” only. H.R. Rep. 80-308,

11   at A191 (1947). We see no indication that Congress intended to engineer a

12   substantive legal change in the statute. See Wong, 575 U.S. at 410; see also 3M

13   Co. (Minn. Mining & Mfg.) v. Browner, 17 F.3d 1453, 1458 (D.C. Cir. 1994)

14   (rejecting a reading of § 2462 that would “treat the Reviser’s rewriting of § 2462

15   as a modification of the statute’s substance” and concluding that “[w]hen the

16   Reviser’s Notes describe the alterations as changes in phraseology, the well-

17   established canon of construction is that the revised statute means only what it

18   meant before 1948”).



                                              13
 1         Even if we were to set aside statutory text and history, however, this case

2    is not “the exceptional one in which a century’s worth of precedent and practice

 3   in American courts rank a time limit as jurisdictional.” Sebelius v. Auburn Reg’l

 4   Med. Ctr., 568 U.S. 145, 155 (2013) (quotation marks omitted). Until now, we

 5   have not squarely addressed the issue in a precedential opinion, although some

 6   of our sister circuits have treated § 2462 as a nonjurisdictional statute without

 7   specifically holding that it is. See, e.g., Arch Min. Corp. v. Babbitt, 104 F.3d 660,

 8   670 (4th Cir. 1997) (noting that the district court had jurisdiction to consider a

 9   § 2462 statute of limitations defense as an affirmative defense); FEC v. Williams,

10   104 F.3d 237, 240 (9th Cir. 1996) (noting that § 2462 “is subject to equitable

11   tolling”). We see no reason to diverge from the consistent view of our sister

12   circuits. “Neither the text nor the context nor the legislative history indicates

13   (much less does so plainly) that Congress meant to enact something other than a

14   standard time bar” or to engineer a substantive legal change in the statute.

15   Wong, 575 U.S. at 410. We therefore hold that § 2462 is a nonjurisdictional

16   statute of limitations, that the parties’ tolling agreement was enforceable, and

17   that the District Court had the authority to hear this case.

 1                                             II

 2         Next, Fowler argues that the SEC improperly brought and pursued a

 3   suitability claim rather than a churning claim arising from his excessive trading

4    in his customers’ accounts. As we previously noted, the SEC claimed that Fowler

5    had violated his reasonable-basis suitability obligation under FINRA’s rules.

6    Fowler’s conduct contravened this suitability obligation, the SEC alleged,

7    because he “knew or recklessly disregarded that the strategy [he] knowingly

8    recommended—a high-cost strategy of excessive in-and-out trading—was bound

9    to lose money and was not suitable for [his] customers.” App’x 17.

10         Fowler insists that the SEC’s suit should have been limited to a churning

11   claim rather than a reasonable-basis suitability claim. Of course, this argument

12   assumes that churning claims and suitability claims arise from mutually

13   exclusive events. They do not. The various securities law provisions do not

14   cover “different, mutually exclusive, spheres of conduct. . . . [The Supreme]

15   Court and the [SEC] have long recognized considerable overlap among the

16   subsections of . . . Rule [10b-5] and related provisions of the securities laws.”

17   Lorenzo v. SEC, 139 S. Ct. 1094, 1102 (2019). So brokers may, in the same course

18   of conduct, make unsuitable trading recommendations to their customers while



                                               15
 1   at the same time actively churning customer accounts just to generate fees and

 2   commissions. In other words, churning claims and suitability claims can arise

 3   from the same general set of facts.

 4         The SEC had an adequate basis to pursue its suitability claim under the

 5   circumstances of this case. The agency itself has long held that “excessive

 6   trading . . . can violate [FINRA] suitability standards by representing an

 7   unsuitable frequency of trading.” Pinchas, Exchange Act Release No. 41816, 1999

 8   WL 680044, at *6 (Sept. 1, 1999). And even if pursuing a suitability claim on the

 9   facts of this case represented a novel approach, novelty is not error. Indeed,

10   Fowler has never suggested that the SEC failed to state a reasonable-basis

11   suitability claim; instead, he has merely asserted that a churning claim was more

12   appropriate.

13         For these reasons, we find no error in the District Court’s decision to allow

14   the SEC to proceed to trial with its reasonable-basis suitability claim.

15         In his final challenge to the jury verdict, Fowler suggests that the SEC

16   failed to prove that he controlled the customer accounts. There is no doubt that a

17   churning claim requires a plaintiff to prove that the defendant exercised actual or

18   de facto control over the churned accounts. See Newburger, Loeb & Co. v.



                                              16
 1   Gross, 563 F.2d 1057, 1069–70 (2d Cir. 1977). Even the SEC, which has the burden

 2   of proof on this issue, appears to agree. See Calabro, Exchange Act Release No.

 3   9798, 2015 WL 3439152, at *1 (May 29, 2015). But what is before us is a suitability

 4   claim, not a churning claim. And a suitability claim is different: it fundamentally

 5   rests on the broker’s recommendation to a potential or actual customer rather

 6   than on any actual trading activity. The SEC was therefore not required to show

 7   that Fowler controlled any account in order to prove its suitability claim. See

 8   Brown v. E.F. Hutton Grp., Inc., 991 F.2d 1020, 1031 (2d Cir. 1993). The District

 9   Court’s ruling to that effect was correct.

 5         We first address Fowler’s argument that the civil penalties in this case run

 6   afoul of the penalty sections of the Securities Act, which provide for three tiers of

 7   civil penalties. See 15 U.S.C. § 77t(d)(2). The most serious of these, a Tier III civil

 8   penalty, sets a maximum penalty “for each . . . violation” that involved “fraud,

 9   deceit, manipulation, or deliberate or reckless disregard of a regulatory

10   requirement,” and “directly or indirectly resulted in substantial losses or created

11   a significant risk of substantial losses to other persons.” Id. at § 77t(d)(2)(C). In

12   those cases, the maximum penalty is “$100,000 for a natural person” or “the

13   gross amount of pecuniary gain to such defendant as a result of the violation,”

14   (what we refer to as the gain clause). Id. The SEC adjusts the $100,000 maximum

15   penalty for inflation based on the date of the violations. See 17 C.F.R. § 201.1001.

16   In Fowler’s case, the penalty was adjusted to $150,000. See id. at § 201.1001 tbl.I.

17         The term “violation” is not defined by the statutory scheme. In the course

18   of determining the appropriate unit of violation, the District Court observed that



                                               20
 1   “Fowler selected his victims for this conduct individually.” Sp. App’x 86. As a

 2   result, it concluded that “treating his treatment of each of his defrauded

 3   customers as a separate violation best effectuates the purposes of the statute.”

 4   Id. This conclusion is entirely plausible. In SEC v. Pentagon Capital

 5   Management PLC, for example, we determined that it was not error to

6    “calculat[e] the maximum penalty by counting each [violative] trade as a

7    separate violation,” let alone each customer victimized by the trades. 725 F.3d

 8   279, 288 n.7 (2d Cir. 2013). Fowler argues that Pentagon Capital is inapposite

 9   because the total penalty awarded there fell within the alternative statutory

10   maximum in the gain clause. See 15 U.S.C. § 77t(d)(2)(C). Here, by contrast, the

11   District Court imposed iterative civil penalties of $100,000 (adjusted for inflation)

12   per customer under § 77t(d)(2)(C).

13         The difference between the two statutory caps in § 77t(d)(2)(C) is irrelevant

14   to this appeal. The question before us is whether each defrauded customer can

15   be counted as a separate “violation” under the statute. In Pentagon Capital, we

16   actually emphasized the statute’s use of the phrase “each such violation” to

17   conclude that each of the trades in that case was an appropriate unit of violation.

18   See 725 F.3d at 288 n.7.

 3   remedies, . . . and its choice of remedies is reviewable for abuse of discretion.”

 4   SEC v. Sourlis, 851 F.3d 139, 146 (2d Cir. 2016) (quotation marks omitted). Here,

 5   the District Court adequately explained that a per-trade penalty “would be so

 6   substantial” that Fowler would not “reasonably be capable” of paying it. Sp.

 7   App’x 87. 9 And in opposing the SEC’s post-trial motion for remedies below,

 8   Fowler acknowledged that the jury’s findings of liability were customer-specific

 9   rather than based on specific trades, even as he insisted that he was merely

10   “carrying out a single scheme.” App’x 168–69 (quotation marks omitted). For

11   these reasons, we will not second-guess the District Court’s discretionary

12   decision to resort to a per-customer unit of violation to determine the civil

13   penalty in this case.

16         Finally, Fowler urges us to focus on a district court’s authority to impose a

17   third-tier penalty “for each . . . violation” only if the “violation directly or

18   indirectly resulted in substantial losses or created a significant risk of substantial



                                                23
 1   losses to other persons,” 15 U.S.C. § 77t(d)(2)(C)(II) (emphasis added). The “clear

 2   implication” of the use of the plural “persons,” he claims, “is that when multiple

 3   investors are affected, the appropriate remedy is to upgrade the penalty from

 4   Second to Third Tier, not multiply it for each affected investor.” Appellant’s Br.

 5   34.

 6         Fowler’s reading would foreclose a Tier III penalty whenever there is a

 7   single victim regardless of the type or level of harm. The interpretation also

 8   contradicts the basic principle that “unless the context indicates otherwise . . .

 9   words importing the plural include the singular.” 1 U.S.C. § 1. Recall that the

10   maximum penalties in the Tier III provision describe the offender as either a

11   “natural person” or “any other person.” 15 U.S.C. § 77t(d)(2)(C). In order to

12   distinguish from the offender, the statute refers to “other persons” to indicate

13   that Tier III penalties do not include losses the offender suffered. When viewed

14   in the context of the entire statute, therefore, the term “other persons” means

15   “anyone other than the offender,” not “multiple victims.”

16         Fowler also asks us to consider that the monetary penalty that the SEC can

17   impose in SEC administrative proceedings under the Investment Company Act,

18   15 U.S.C. § 80a-9(d)(2)(C) is based on each “act or omission,” not each victim.



                                               24
 1   But this compares statutory apples to statutory oranges. Unlike the statute at

 2   issue here, the Investment Company Act provision on which Fowler relies

 3   permits the SEC to impose administrative penalties on aiders and abettors. See

 4   15 U.S.C. § 80a-9(d)(1)(A)(ii). The term “act or omission” in that context only

 5   makes clear that the SEC can impose penalties specifically for each act of aiding

 6   or abetting.

 7         In sum, we are not persuaded that the District Court was barred from

 8   treating each defrauded customer as a separate unit of violation in imposing civil

 9   penalties. We see no need to set a maximum number of violations that would be

10   appropriate on these facts. We conclude only that the District Court did not

11   abuse its wide discretion in finding at least thirteen violations here.

12                                             B

13         Fowler’s constitutional argument fares no better than his statutory

14   challenge. Analogizing to punitive damages, he submits that his civil penalty is

15   so grossly disproportionate to the amount he was ordered to disgorge (fifteen

16   times) that it violated his rights under the Fifth and Eighth Amendments. See,

17   e.g., Pac. Mut. Life Ins. Co. v. Haslip, 499 U.S. 1, 23–24 (1991) (noting that a




                                               25
 1   punitive damage award more than quadruple the compensatory damage award

 2   was “close to the [constitutional] line”).

 3         We have not previously held that the civil penalty for a securities fraud

 4   offense needs to be proportional to the disgorgement amount. Instead, several

 5   factors determine an appropriate civil penalty award: “(1) the egregiousness of

6    the defendant’s conduct; (2) the degree of the defendant’s scienter; (3) whether

7    the defendant’s conduct created substantial losses or the risk of substantial losses

8    to other persons; (4) whether the defendant’s conduct was isolated or recurrent;

9    and (5) whether the penalty should be reduced due to the defendant’s

10   demonstrated current and future financial condition.” SEC v. Rajaratnam, 918

11   F.3d 36, 44 (2d Cir. 2019).

12         Fowler has never said that he is unable to pay the civil penalty. The

13   District Court nevertheless considered various factors to decide whether the

14   penalty was proportionate to the gravity of his offense. The District Court found

15   that a significant penalty was warranted against Fowler because “[h]is conduct

16   was egregious.” Sp. App’x 85. It especially noted that Fowler “took advantage

17   of the relative lack of sophistication of some of his clients to bilk them”; that he

18   “acted with scienter,” continuing his misconduct even in the face of multiple



                                                  26
 1   customer complaints about his investment strategy; and that his conduct resulted

 2   in “substantial” losses for customers and was “recurrent.” Sp. App’x 85–86. Its

 3   conclusion that the penalty was thus proportionate to Fowler’s conduct was not

 4   error, and the civil penalties imposed in this case fell within constitutional

5    bounds.

6                                              V

 7         Fowler also asks us to vacate the District Court’s disgorgement award and

 8   remand to allow it to recalculate the amount of disgorgement in light of Liu v.

 9   SEC, 140 S. Ct. 1936 (2020), which held in relevant part that “courts must deduct

10   legitimate expenses before ordering disgorgement under § 78u(d)(5).” 140 S. Ct.

11   at 1950. Consistent with Liu, the District Court deducted the portion of Fowler’s

12   commissions that were transferred to J.D. Nicholas and Dean in the ordinary

13   course. But Fowler failed then and fails now to identify any other legitimate

14   business expenses that the District Court should have deducted in light of Liu.

15         In general, “[t]he amount of disgorgement ordered need only be a

16   reasonable approximation of profits causally connected to the violation.” SEC v.

17   Razmilovic, 738 F.3d 14, 31 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted). If the

18   disgorgement amount is generally reasonable, “any risk of uncertainty” about



                                              27
 1   the amount “fall[s] on the wrongdoer whose illegal conduct created that

 2   uncertainty.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). Fowler failed to identify any

 3   additional “legitimate” business expenses that, consistent with Liu, should have

 4   been deducted from an otherwise reasonable disgorgement amount. Yet it was

 5   his burden to do so. We therefore decline to remand to the District Court on this

 6   issue.

 7            Relatedly, the parties agree that the District Court miscalculated the

 8   disgorgement award by ordering Fowler to disgorge more postage fees—that is,

 9   the $65 and then $49.95 per trade fee, of which Fowler was to receive a portion—

10   than he actually received. The District Court found that Fowler received $27,498

11   in postage fees, and ordered him to disgorge that amount (along with his

12   commissions) because it thought that Fowler received 50 percent of the postage

13   fees charged to the thirteen customers, when in fact he received only $3,005 in

14   postage fees. We need not remand to correct this agreed error. See SEC v.

15   Palmisano, 135 F.3d 860, 863–64 (2d Cir. 1998). Instead, we modify the

16   disgorgement award to $107,591.40, plus prejudgment interest in the amount of

17   $25,891.17.
