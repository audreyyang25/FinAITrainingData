Before: KEARSE, PARKER, and SULLIVAN, Circuit Judges. 3 Plaintiff-Appellant Aaron Rubenstein appeals from an order of the United 5 States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Engelmayer, J.) 6 dismissing his complaint under Section 16(b) of the Securities Exchange Act for 7 failure to state a claim. We hold that an investment management agreement 8 delegating discretionary investment authority to an investment advisor is not an 9 agreement to trade in the securities of an issuer and, therefore, is not a 10 standalone basis for membership in an insider group. Moreover, we hold that 11 such an investment advisor’s client does not become an insider group member 12 simply because the advisor has filed a Schedule 13D or deputized a director on 13 an issuer’s board. Consequently, clients who have not entered an issuer-specific 14 trading agreement are not liable for disgorgement of short-swing profits solely 15 by virtue of their investment advisor’s insider status. 16

BARRINGTON D. PARKER, Circuit Judge: 1
Plaintiff-Appellant Aaron Rubenstein appeals from an order of the United 2 States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Engelmayer, J.) 3 dismissing, under Rule 12(b)(6), his complaint alleging violations of Section 16(b) 4 of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 (the “‘34 Act”). The issue presented is 5 whether a client of an investment advisor became a member of a Section 13(d) 6 group with his investment advisor and the advisor’s other clients merely because 7 he and the other clients had delegated discretionary investment authority to the 8 advisor and the advisor had purchased for the client’s account shares of the same 9 issuer that was the subject of the advisor’s Schedule 13D filing. We agree with 10 the court below that the client did not thereby become a member of a group and 11 that the client was not obliged to disgorge his short-swing profits. Accordingly, 12 we affirm the order of the district court. 13 To prevent insiders of a securities issuer from trading on material non-14 public information, Section 16(b) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 imposes 15 strict liability on certain insiders of an issuer, requiring them to disgorge to the 16 issuer any profits they realize from short-swing trading in the issuer’s securities. 17 As defined by Section 16(b), short-swing trading is “any purchase and sale, or 18

any sale and purchase, of any equity security of such issuer . . . within any period 1 of less than six months . . . irrespective of any intention on the part of [the 2 insider].” 15 U.S.C. § 78p(b). Among the insiders subject to the rule are directors 3 and officers of the issuer, as well as “[e]very person who is directly or indirectly 4 the beneficial owner of more than 10 percent of any class of any equity security” 5 of the issuer. Id.1 Section 16(b) is known as the “short-swing profit rule.” 6 In addition to requiring individual statutory insiders to disgorge short-7 swing profits, the ’34 Act provides for “group” liability. Section 13(d)(3) provides 8 that “[w]hen two or more persons act as a . . . group for the purpose of acquiring, 9 holding, or disposing of securities of an issuer,” the group shall be deemed a 10

Defendants moved to dismiss the complaint and the district court granted 1 the motion. The court observed that the touchstone of a group under Section 2 13(d) is that its members combined in furtherance of a common objective. The 3 court held that the complaint did not plausibly allege a common objective 4 between the IVA defendants, who had a control purpose, and John Doe, whose 5 DeVry shares had been purchased for his account on a discretionary basis by his 6 investment manager. In other words, the court held that Rubenstein had not 7 plausibly pled the existence of a Section 13(d) agreement. Rubenstein v. Int’l Value 8 Advisers, LLC, 363 F. Supp. 3d 379, 394-95 (S.D.N.Y. 2019). 9 The court further concluded that the applicable Section 13(d) rule, in 10 addition to requiring an agreement, required its members to have acted together 11 for the purpose of acquiring not just any security but the securities of “an issuer,” 12 meaning a particular issuer. Because the complaint did not plausibly allege that 13 this had occurred, the court determined that John Doe did not become a member 14 of a 13(d) group and could not be required to return his DeVry profits. Id. at 392. 15 This appeal followed. “We review a district court’s grant of a motion to 16 dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6) de novo.” Edwards v. Sequoia Fund, Inc., 938 F.3d 8, 12 17

(2d Cir. 2019); see also Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009); Bell Atl. Corp. v. 1 Twombly, 550 U.S. 544, 555-56 (2007). 2
DISCUSSION 3
Resolution of this appeal turns on whether John Doe was a member of an 4 insider group. We hold that he was not. Our holding follows from the text and 5 structure of the ’34 Act and its implementing regulations, as well as the 6 legislative purpose behind the short-swing profit rule. Moreover, we are 7 unmoved by Rubenstein’s arguments to the contrary, which are based either on a 8 misunderstanding of certain regulatory exemptions or on erroneous legal 9 positions. 10
I. 11
A Section 13(d) group is formed “[w]hen two or more persons agree to act 12 together for the purpose of acquiring, holding, voting or disposing of equity 13 securities of an issuer.” 17 C.F.R. § 240.13d-5(b)(1). The group is then deemed to 14 have acquired beneficial ownership of all equity securities of that issuer 15 beneficially owned by any group members. If the group’s collective holdings 16 exceed 10% of any class of the issuer’s outstanding equity securities, then each 17 group member is subject to the short-swing profit rule. See id. § 240.16a-1(a)(3). 18

Rubenstein does not allege that any defendants are liable for short-swing 1 profits except John Doe, who realized the profits in an account managed for him 2 by IVA. It is not disputed that the other defendants were statutory insiders who 3 would have been obligated to return short-swing profits had they realized them. 4 They were required to file, and did file, Schedule 13Ds. 5 Rubenstein relies principally on the theory that IVA’s clients became 6 members of a group with their investment advisor and its other clients when 7 they signed investment management agreements delegating discretionary 8 trading authority to IVA. We do not agree. Rubenstein’s theory is incompatible 9 with the text of the ’34 Act and its implementing regulations. Both provide that a 10 group is formed when multiple individuals agree to act together “for the 11 purpose of acquiring, holding, or disposing of securities of an issuer.” 15 U.S.C. § 12 78m(d)(3) (emphasis added); see also 17 C.F.R. § 240.13d-5(b)(1). The regulations 13 then deem the group members “to have acquired beneficial ownership . . . of all 14 equity securities of that issuer beneficially owned by any [group members].” 17 15 C.F.R. § 240.13d-5(b)(1) (emphasis added). 16 We straightforwardly conclude that these provisions impose liability only 17 when insiders enter an agreement, as defined in 17 C.F.R. § 13d-5(b)(1), to trade 18

the securities of a particular issuer. In American Standard, Inc. v. Crane Co., we said 1 that because Section 16(b) “speaks of ‘such issuer’ in the singular,” there is “no 2 room for a grammatical construction that would convert the singular into a 3 plural.” 510 F.2d 1043, 1058 (2d Cir. 1974); see also Roth v. Jennings, 489 F.3d 499, 4 510-12 (2d Cir. 2007); Morales v. Quintel Entm’t, Inc., 249 F.3d 115, 125-26 (2d Cir. 5 2001); Morales v. Freund, 163 F.3d 763, 767 (2d Cir. 1999). 6 Rubenstein does not allege the existence of any such agreement. He admits 7 that the only agreement between the IVA defendants and John Doe is an 8 investment management agreement under which John Doe gave the IVA 9 defendants discretionary authority to trade securities in his account and agreed 10 to pay them a fee for these services. Neither DeVry nor any other issuer was 11 identified as one whose stock IVA might purchase for John Doe’s account. In 12 addition, the investment management agreement did not address whether, or to 13 what extent, the IVA defendants might purchase or sell the same securities for 14 the other accounts they managed; nor did the investment management 15 agreement touch on the subject of whether the IVA defendants might seek to 16 influence or control an issuer whose shares might be in an account they 17 managed. Consequently, Rubenstein is simply wrong when he contends that the 18

investment management agreement constituted an “agreement” to trade in 1 DeVry securities under Section 13(d) and 17 C.F.R. § 240.13d-5(b)(1). 2 An investment advisory client does not form a group with its investment 3 advisor by merely entering into an investment advisory relationship. Nor does 4 an investor become a member of a group solely because his or her advisor caused 5 other (or all) of its clients to invest in securities of the same issuer. And 6 Rubenstein points us to nothing else that might constitute an “agreement” or 7 demonstrate a “common objective” to trade in the securities of “an issuer.” 8
II. 9
In addition to its incompatibility with the text of the statutes and rules, 10 Rubenstein’s interpretation does not serve the purpose of the statute, which is 11 designed to “prevent a group of persons who seek to pool their voting or other 12 interests in the securities of an issuer from evading the provisions of the statute 13 because no one individual owns more than 10 percent of the securities.” H.R. 14 Rep. No. 90-1711 (1968), as reprinted in 1968 U.S.C.C.A.N. 2811, 2818; see also 15 Quintel, 249 F.3d at 123 (“[T]he purpose of the provision was to prevent evasion 16 of the disclosure requirement.”). Rubenstein has never alleged that IVA’s clients 17 sought to pool their shares to evade the requirements of the statute. See generally 18

Magma Power Co. v. Dow Chem. Co., 136 F.3d 316, 320 (2d Cir. 1998) (holding that 1 courts should evaluate short-swing transactions pragmatically to determine 2 whether they serve as a “vehicle for the evil which Congress sought to prevent” 3 (quoting Kern Cty. Land Co. v. Occidental Petroleum Corp., 411 U.S. 582, 594 4 (1973))). 5 Nonetheless, Rubenstein offers a variety of policy arguments in support of 6 his position, none of which persuades us. For example, he contends that our 7 holding will facilitate illicit insider trading. Section 16(b) is designed to prevent 8 abuse of inside information by prohibiting a narrow class of trades that Congress 9 viewed as particularly likely to be based on such information. Rubenstein voices 10 the concern that our holding will enable investment managers to evade Section 11 16(b) and to abuse inside information by trading in client funds rather than their 12 own funds because client funds may not be subject to disgorgement. He argues 13 that if clients are not members of a group with their advisor, then their advisor 14 will be able to trade its clients’ shares using inside information and to earn 15 profits from those trades. 16 Because of the strict liability imposed by Section 16(b), the Supreme Court 17 has cautioned against exceeding the “narrowly drawn limits” on the class of 18

corporate insiders who may be subject to the statute. Gollust v. Mendell, 501 U.S. 1 115, 122 (1991) (quoting Foremost-McKesson, Inc. v. Provident Sec. Co., 423 U.S. 232, 2 251 (1976)); accord Reliance Elec. Co. v. Emerson Elec. Co., 404 U.S. 418, 427 (1972). 3 Accordingly, we may not expand the boundaries of the statute merely to address 4 Rubenstein’s concerns. 5 In any case, Congress has chosen to address Rubenstein’s concerns in other 6 ways. Exempting certain client profits from Section 16(b) does not insulate 7 investment advisors from liability under the more general anti-fraud provisions 8 of the ‘34 Act: Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5. If IVA had improperly used inside 9 information to trade in its clients’ accounts, it could be subject to Rule 10b-5, 10 regardless of whether its clients were part of an insider group. Section 16(b) 11 addresses only a narrow class of potential insider trading. By contrast, Rule 10b-5 12 addresses a broader sphere, including the insider trading that Rubenstein asks 13 Section 16(b) to police. Trading that passes muster under Section 16(b) may not 14 do so under Rule 10b-5. Rubenstein’s fear that our holding will offer a safe 15 harbor to investment managers engaged in insider trading is consequently 16 unwarranted. Suffice it to say that his complaint contains no allegations that Rule 17

10b-5 has been violated, and that provision plays no part in our resolution of this 1 case. 2 Rubenstein next complains that our holding will undermine Section 13(d)’s 3 disclosure requirement.3 Section 13(d) requires persons who are the beneficial 4 owners of more than 5% of a company’s shares to file a disclosure statement with 5 the SEC. 15 U.S.C. § 78m(d). The requirement is designed to alert investors when 6 shareholders acquire potentially controlling interests in corporations. Rubenstein 7 argues that our holding will enable investment advisors to exercise control over 8 companies without disclosing their interests if they distribute shares among their 9 clients’ accounts such that the advisors’ own holdings never reach the 5% 10 threshold. 11 However, this argument overlooks the fact that investment advisors may 12 qualify as beneficial owners of shares in their clients’ accounts, for purposes of 13 Section 13(d), regardless of whether they form an insider group with their clients. 14 When their clients’ holdings in a security collectively reach the 5% threshold, the 15 advisors may be subject to Section 13(d)’s reporting requirements. This is 16 because Rule 13d-3 defines beneficial ownership not by pecuniary interest in a 17

IV. 1
In the absence of any agreement to trade in the securities of an issuer, 2 Rubenstein argues for a theory of implied agreement. IVA was a statutory 3 insider that filed a Schedule 13D with the SEC disclosing its status. Rubenstein 4 contends that by silently acquiescing in their advisor’s trading for their benefit 5 after the advisor filed that form, IVA’s clients implicitly agreed to trade in the 6 securities of DeVry as members of the insider group. 7 We find no support for this theory in the relevant statutes or regulations. 8 Rubenstein would have us treat all investors as though they were conscious of 9 the securities held by their advisors’ other clients and would mandate that they 10 tailor their investment decisions to those other clients’ trades. He would further 11 require them to monitor their advisors’ activity on various corporate boards and 12 to assess whether their advisors may have adopted a control purpose with 13 respect to particular issuers. Such requirements are impracticable ones that are 14 not contemplated by the securities laws. Section 16(b) is not designed to threaten 15 liability based on the trades of other investors to whom a defendant’s only 16 connection is sharing an investment advisor. 17

Rubenstein claims that these difficulties can be avoided if investment 1 advisors adopt compliance procedures that prevent short-swing trading. 2 However, Rubenstein seeks to hold advisors’ clients liable, and those clients may 3 have no control over their advisor’s compliance procedures. Rubenstein’s 4 proposed theory of liability would demand that clients, who may be 5 unsophisticated individual investors, adopt procedures for monitoring the 6 investments of their advisors’ other clients. Rubenstein would hold a retiree on 7 the beach in Florida liable when he fails to conduct an ongoing analysis of his 8 IRA manager’s trading in other clients’ accounts. We decline to go down this 9 road. 10
V. 11
Rubenstein’s final theory of liability, raised for the first time on appeal, is 12 that IVA acted as an agent and director by deputization for its clients. We see no 13 merit in this theory. To be sure, there is some support in our precedent for the 14 theory that a statutory insider may not escape liability by merely delegating 15 investment responsibility to an agent. See, e.g., Huppe v. WPCS Int’l Inc., 670 F.3d 16 214, 221-22 (2d Cir. 2012). That is, an insider principal cannot shed its insider 17 status by transferring trading authority to a non-insider agent. For example, the 18

owner of 10% of the outstanding shares of a security may not delegate authority 1 over 5% to an agent and thereby claim to be a 5% shareholder who is not subject 2 to Section 16(b). No authority, however, suggests that a non-insider principal 3 may unknowingly inherit the insider status of its agent. 4 Rubenstein also points us to a “director by deputization” theory, under 5 which an investor becomes a statutory insider when it deputizes an individual to 6 serve as its representative on an issuer’s board of directors. See generally Blau v. 7 Lehman, 368 U.S. 403, 408-10 (1962); Roth ex rel. Beacon Power Corp. v. Perseus, 8 L.L.C., 522 F.3d 242, 246 (2d Cir. 2008). IVA argues that, under this theory, IVA’s 9 clients were insiders because they hired IVA as their investment advisor and IVA 10 then appointed a director to DeVry’s board. Although this appointment may 11 have rendered IVA a statutory insider, it did not render its clients insiders. IVA’s 12 clients did not deputize anyone as a director. It is unlikely that most of these 13 clients were even aware that their advisor had deputized a director on the DeVry 14 board. And even if some clients were so aware, there is no indication that they 15 agreed to the deputization. In any event, we conclude that further discussion of 16 these theories is not appropriate because they were not raised in the court below. 17

In sum, group liability under Sections 13(d) and 16(b) requires that group 1 members “agree to act together for the purpose of acquiring, holding, voting or 2 disposing of equity securities of an issuer.” 17 C.F.R. § 240.13d-5(b)(1); see also 15 3 U.S.C. § 78m(d)(3). Because Rubenstein’s interpretation would read the 4 requirements of agreement and issuer-specificity out of the statute, we reject it. 5
CONCLUSION 6
For the foregoing reasons, the order of the district court is AFFIRMED. 7
