23 KEARSE, Circuit Judge:
24 Defendant David Parse appeals from a judgment entered in the United States District
25 Court for the Southern District of New York following a jury trial before William H. Pauley III, Judge,
26 convicting him on one count of mail fraud, in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1341 and 2, and on one count
27 of corruptly endeavoring to obstruct and impede the administration of the internal revenue laws, in
1 violation of 26 U.S.C. § 7212(a). Parse was sentenced principally to 42 months' imprisonment, to be
2 followed by three years of supervised release, and was ordered to forfeit $1,000,000 and to pay
3 $115,830,267 in restitution. On appeal, he contends principally that he should have been granted a
4 new trial on the ground of newly discovered evidence of juror bias; he challenges the district court's
5 denial of his posttrial motion pursuant to Rule 33 of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure for that
6 relief, made jointly with his codefendants against whom the jury had returned verdicts of guilty, in
7 light of postverdict evidence establishing that one of the jurors had lied on voir dire, concealed
8 relevant information, and was actually biased against the defendants. The district court granted the
9 new-trial motion of Parse's codefendants; but it denied the motion of Parse, finding that his attorneys
10 either knew of the juror's misconduct prior to the verdict or failed to act with reasonable diligence
11 based on the information they had, and that Parse had thus waived his right to an impartial jury. Parse
12 also contends that the evidence was insufficient to support the jury's verdicts against him, and he
13 challenges certain of the instructions given to the jury. Finding merit in Parse's challenge to the denial
14 of his Rule 33 motion, but not in his sufficiency challenges, we vacate the judgment and remand for
15 a new trial of the counts on which Parse was convicted.
16 I. BACKGROUND
17 Parse and several others were indicted in 2009 and were ultimately charged with one
18 count of conspiracy to defraud the United States and to commit mail fraud, wire fraud, and tax
19 evasion, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 371, and with multiple substantive counts of tax evasion and other
20 tax-related offenses in connection with the creation of a series of tax shelters "designed and marketed
1 by [a law firm and an accounting firm] to take advantage of Internal Revenue Code . . . loopholes so
2 taxpayers could claim non-economic tax losses to avoid taxes they otherwise would have owed" (Parse
3 brief on appeal at 7). Parse was a broker employed by an investment banking firm that executed
4 transactions for implementation of the shelters.
5 In the spring of 2011, Parse was tried along with four of his codefendants: Paul
6 Daugerdas and Donna Guerin, who were attorneys at the law firm; Denis Field, a member of the
7 accounting firm; and Craig Brubaker, a broker at the investment bank that employed Parse. Following
8 a three-month trial at which 41 witnesses testified and some 1,300 exhibits were admitted, the jury on
9 May 24 returned a split verdict. It found Parse guilty on two counts--the substantive mail fraud and
10 obstruction charges described above--and found him not guilty on the other four counts against him. 
11 (See Trial Transcript ("Tr.") 9153-54, 9159-63.) It found Daugerdas guilty on all 24 counts against
12 him; found Guerin guilty on all 12 counts against her; found Field guilty on all 7 counts against him;
13 and found Brubaker not guilty on any of the counts against him. (See id. at 9153-63.)

22 C. The Granting of a New Trial to Daugerdas, Guerin, and Field
23 The district court, discussing the relevant Supreme Court cases, including McDonough
24 Power Equipment, Inc. v. Greenwood, 464 U.S. 548 (1984) ("McDonough"), and courts of appeals
25 cases, noted that the Sixth Amendment guarantees an accused the right to a trial by an impartial jury. 
26 "An impartial jury is one in which every juror is 'capable and willing to decide the case solely on the
27 evidence before [her].'" Daugerdas, 867 F.Supp.2d at 470 (quoting McDonough, 464 U.S. at 554). 
28 "Voir dire plays an essential role in protecting the right to a trial by an impartial jury," and as
29 questioning on voir dire seeks to ferret out actual or potential biases on the part of potential jurors, a
30 "juror's dishonesty during voir dire undermines a defendant's right to a fair trial." Daugerdas, 867
1 F.Supp.2d at 468.
2 "If the answers to the questions [during voir dire] are willfully evasive or
3 knowingly untrue, the talesman, when accepted, is a juror in name only. . . . 
4 His relation to the court and to the parties is tainted in its origin; it is a mere
5 pretense and sham." Clark v. United States, 289 U.S. 1, 11, 53 S.Ct. 465, 77
6 L.Ed. 993 (1933); see also McDonough, 464 U.S. at 554, 104 S.Ct. 845 ("The
7 necessity of truthful answers by prospective jurors if [voir dire] is to serve its
8 purpose is obvious."). Thus, a juror who lies her way onto a jury is not really
9 a juror at all; she is an interloper akin "to a stranger who sneaks into the jury
10 room." Dyer v. Calderon, 151 F.3d 970, 983 (9th Cir. 1998) (en banc). 
11 "Justice must satisfy the appearance of justice." Offutt v. United States, 348
12 U.S. 11, 14, 75 S.Ct. 11, 99 L.Ed. 11 (1954).
13 Daugerdas, 867 F.Supp.2d at 468; see also id. ("where a juror deliberately conceals information that,

16 (quoting United States v. Colombo, 869 F.2d 149, 151 (2d Cir. 1989) ("Colombo"))).

7 In contrast, the district court concluded that Parse had waived his right to a new trial.

25 The right to challenge the panel or to challenge a particular juror may

13 A. The Denial of Parse's Motion for a New Trial
14 Rule 33 of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure provides, in pertinent part, that
15 "[u]pon the defendant's motion, the court may vacate any judgment and grant a new trial if the interest
16 of justice so requires." Fed. R. Crim. P. 33(a). Such a motion, if made within three years after the
17 verdict or finding of guilty, may be based on "newly discovered evidence," Fed. R. Crim. P. 33(b); but
18 such motions are "not favored," United States v. Diaz, 176 F.3d 52, 106 (2d Cir.) (internal quotation
19 marks omitted), cert. denied, 528 U.S. 875 (1999); United States v. Spencer, 4 F.3d 115, 118 (2d Cir.
1 1993) ("Spencer"); see, e.g., United States v. Slutsky, 514 F.2d 1222, 1225 (2d Cir. 1975).
2 Evidence is not new if the defendant knew of it prior to trial, see, e.g., United States
3 v. Mayo, 14 F.3d 128, 132 (2d Cir. 1994) ("Mayo"), and is not considered "newly discovered" if, with
4 the exercise of reasonable diligence, it could have been discovered before or during the trial, see, e.g.,
5 United States v. Siddiqi, 959 F.2d 1167, 1173 (2d Cir. 1992) ("Siddiqi") (the defendant must "make[]
6 a showing that the evidence is in fact 'new', i.e., it could not have been discovered, exercising due
7 diligence, before or during trial"); Spencer, 4 F.3d at 119. A defendant is not entitled to remain silent
8 while having knowledge of facts of an impropriety that would entitle him to relief and to invoke that
9 impropriety only later if the result of the proceeding is unsatisfactory to him. See generally Puckett
10 v. United States, 556 U.S. 129, 134 (2009) ("If a litigant believes that an error has occurred (to his
11 detriment) during a federal judicial proceeding, he must object in order to preserve the issue. . . . 
12 [T]he contemporaneous-objection rule prevents a litigant from 'sandbagging' the court--remaining
13 silent about his objection and belatedly raising the error only if the case does not conclude in his
14 favor." (other internal quotation marks omitted)).
15 Thus, a motion for a new trial based on a claim of newly discovered evidence generally
16 should not be granted unless, inter alia, the evidence was in fact newly discovered after trial, and facts
17 are alleged from which the court can infer the exercise of due diligence on the part of the movant to
18 obtain the evidence prior to the end of the trial. See, e.g., United States v. Ragland, 375 F.2d 471, 475
19 (2d Cir. 1967) ("To give an accused a second trial each time he doubts, after an unfavorable verdict,
20 the objectivity of jurors, would seriously impede the processes of justice."), cert. denied, 390 U.S. 925
21 (1968); United States v. Diaz-Albertini, 772 F.2d 654, 656 (10th Cir. 1985) (where a juror failed to
22 disclose his close ties with law enforcement officials and a new trial was granted to the defendant wife
1 who had no knowledge of that nondisclosure, affirming the denial of a new trial to the defendant
2 husband, whose attorney testified that when he was informed of the nondisclosure prior to trial, "he
3 decided at th[at] time" that he would later raise the issue "should we have a conviction" (internal
4 quotation marks omitted)), cert. denied, 484 U.S. 822 (1987); id. at 657 (a "litigant cannot transform
5 a tactical decision to withhold the information from the court's attention into a trump card to be played
6 only if it becomes expedient"); United States v. Bolinger, 837 F.2d 436, 439 (11th Cir.) (affirming
7 denial of a new trial where defendant's attorney learned of juror misconduct during trial and
8 "gamble[d] on the jury rather than inform[ing] the court of the problem in time to allow the court to
9 determine if corrective action was possible prior to verdict"), cert. denied, 486 U.S. 1009 (1988);
10 United States v. Morris, 977 F.2d 677, 686 (1st Cir. 1992) ("We will not allow counsel to stand by
11 quietly and gamble on a favorable verdict, only to complain when it turns out to be otherwise."), cert.
12 denied, 507 U.S. 988 (1993).
13 In sum, if the defendant knew prior to the end of trial that a prospective juror had given
14 false voir dire responses and did not then reveal the disqualifying falsehoods or nondisclosures to the
15 court, the "interest of justice," Fed. R. Crim. P. 33(a), generally will not require a new trial.
16 In addition, even if the evidence was newly discovered within the meaning of Rule

20 This Court reviews the denial of a Rule 33 motion for a new trial for abuse of
21 discretion. See, e.g., Greer 285 F.3d at 170; Persico, 645 F.3d at 109; Owen, 500 F.3d at 87-88;
22 Mayo, 14 F.3d at 132; Siddiqi, 959 F.2d at 1173; Parker, 903 F.2d at 103. A court abuses its
23 discretion if (1) it takes an erroneous view of the law, (2) its decision rests on a clearly erroneous
1 finding of fact, or (3) its decision--though not necessarily the product of a legal error or a clearly
2 erroneous factual finding--cannot be located within the range of permissible decisions. See, e.g.,
3 Owen, 500 F.3d at 87-88; United States v. Thorn, 446 F.3d 378, 391 (2d Cir. 2006).
4 1. The Right to an Impartial Jury
5 The right of a defendant in a criminal prosecution to a trial "by an impartial jury" is
6 expressly guaranteed by the Sixth Amendment to the Constitution. U.S. Const. amend VI. "[T]rying
7 an accused before a jury that is actually biased" not only transgresses the express guarantee of the
8 Sixth Amendment but also "violates even the most minimal standards of due process." United States
9 v. Nelson, 277 F.3d 164, 206 (2d Cir.), cert. denied, 537 U.S. 835 (2002); see, e.g., In re Murchison,
10 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955) ("A fair trial in a fair tribunal is a basic requirement of due process.").
11 An impartial jury is one in which all of its members, not just most of them, are free of
12 interest and bias. See, e.g., United States v. Martinez-Salazar, 528 U.S. 304, 316 (2000) (an error "in
13 the seating of any juror who should have been dismissed for cause . . . . would require reversal");
14 Parker v. Gladden, 385 U.S. 363, 366 (1966) (per curiam) (despite state law authorizing conviction
15 by an affirmative vote of 10 jurors, a new trial was required where at least two members of the
16 12-person jury were exposed to unauthorized communications; "petitioner was entitled to be tried by
17 12, not 9 or even 10, impartial and unprejudiced jurors").
18 The voir dire of prospective jurors, who have been placed under oath, "is an important
19 method of protecting a defendant's right to trial by" jurors who are impartial. Colombo, 869 F.2d
20 at 151. A prospective juror who deliberately lies on voir dire is subject to criminal prosecution for
21 perjury pursuant to, e.g., 18 U.S.C. § 1621 ("perjury" includes willfully making a false statement as
1 to a material matter under oath), to possible criminal contempt pursuant to 18 U.S.C. § 401, and to
2 possible substantial restitution claims by the government under 18 U.S.C. § 3663, cf. United States
3 v. Hand, 863 F.2d 1100, 1101-07 (3d Cir. 1988) (where juror was convicted of contempt for
4 misconduct during trial, which necessitated the vacatur of guilty verdicts, affirming order that juror,
5 inter alia, pay restitution to the government in the amount of $46,850). See generally Colombo, 869
6 F.2d at 151. Where the juror has lied for the purpose of securing a seat on the jury--a mission
7 apparently "so powerful as to cause the juror to commit a serious crime"--it "reflect[s] an
8 impermissible partiality on the juror's part." Id. Such conduct
9 not only suggests a view on the merits and/or knowledge of evidentiary facts
10 but is also quite inconsistent with an expectation that a prospective juror will
11 give truthful answers concerning her or his ability to weigh the evidence fairly
12 and obey the instructions of the court. . . . [C]ertainly when possible
13 non-objectivity is secreted and compounded by the deliberate untruthfulness
14 of a potential juror's answer on voir dire, the result is deprivation of the
15 defendant's rights to a fair trial.
16 Id. at 151-52 (footnote omitted) (internal quotation marks omitted). Where the juror has deliberately
17 concealed information, "bias" is to be "presume[d]." Id. at 152; see, e.g., McCoy v. Goldston, 652
18 F.2d 654, 659 (6th Cir. 1981).

3 2. Principles of Waiver
4 We have considerable difficulty, however, with the district court's denial of Parse's
5 Rule 33 motion for a new trial on the premise that Parse "waived his claim for a new trial based on
6 Conrad's alleged misconduct" because, according to the court, "Parse's attorneys knew--or with a
7 modicum of diligence would have known--that Conrad's voir dire testimony was false and
8 misleading." Daugerdas, 867 F.Supp.2d at 476. Preliminarily, we note that Parse points out that the
9 district court made no finding that he himself had any knowledge whatever about Conrad's
10 misrepresentations in voir dire, and he argues that the district court erred in ruling that his right to trial
11 before an impartial jury was waivable by his attorneys without his personal knowledge or
12 acquiescence (see Parse brief on appeal at 43-46). However, we need not reach this issue here, see
13 generally New York v. Hill, 528 U.S. 110, 114 (2000) ("[f]or certain fundamental rights, the defendant
14 must personally make an informed waiver. . . . For other rights, however, waiver may be effected by
15 action of counsel"), because we conclude that the district court erred in its principal rulings with
16 regard to Parse's attorneys. We conclude that to the extent the district court found that Parse's
17 attorneys knew Conrad had lied, that finding is not supported by the record; and to the extent that the
18 court ruled that Parse's right was waived because his attorneys failed to exercise due diligence to learn
19 the facts, that ruling was based on an error of law.
20 "The classic definition of waiver" is the "'intentional relinquishment or abandonment
21 of a known right or privilege.'" Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 439 (1963) (quoting Johnson v. Zerbst,
1 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (emphases ours)); see, e.g., United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 733
2 (1993).
3 A waiver is ordinarily an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a
4 known right or privilege. The determination of whether there has been an
5 intelligent waiver of right to counsel must depend, in each case, upon the
6 particular facts and circumstances surrounding that case . . . .
7 Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. at 464 (emphasis added). A right is not a "known right" unless one not
8 only is aware of the abstract legal principle, but also knows the facts that make that principle
9 applicable to him. See, e.g., Brady v. United States, 397 U.S. 742, 748 (1970) ("Waivers of
10 constitutional rights not only must be voluntary but must be knowing, intelligent acts done with
11 sufficient awareness of the relevant circumstances and likely consequences." (emphasis added)). 
12 Waiver is not to be presumed. Indeed, "[t]here is a presumption against the waiver of constitutional
13 rights . . . ." Brookhart v. Janis, 384 U.S. 1, 4 (1966) (emphasis added); see, e.g., Glasser v. United
14 States, 315 U.S. 60, 70-71 (1942) ("To preserve the protection of the Bill of Rights for hard-pressed
15 defendants, we indulge every reasonable presumption against the waiver of fundamental rights."). The
16 Government bears the burden of proving a defendant's waiver by a preponderance of the evidence. 
17 See United States v. Mastrangelo, 693 F.2d 269, 273-74 (2d Cir. 1982).
18 In Olano, the Supreme Court analyzed the availability of relief to a litigant under Rule
19 52(b) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, which provides that "[a] plain error that affects
20 substantial rights may be considered even though it was not brought to the court's attention," Fed. R.
21 Crim. P. 52(b). The Court made clear that although one may forfeit his right to relief when he fails
22 to object or otherwise bring an error to the court's attention, not every failure to object or to advance
23 a given argument constitutes a waiver:
1 Waiver is different from forfeiture. Whereas forfeiture is the failure to
2 make the timely assertion of a right, waiver is the "intentional relinquishment
3 or abandonment of a known right."
4 507 U.S. at 733 (quoting Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. at 464 (emphasis ours)). "Deviation from a legal
5 rule is 'error' unless the rule has been waived." Olano, 507 U.S. at 732-33. If "a particular right is
6 waivable," and if the defendant has "knowingly and voluntarily" made the choice to forgo that right,
7 the fact that he did not have the benefit of that right "is not error." Id. at 733 (internal quotation marks
8 omitted). On the other hand, the Olano Court explained,
9 [m]ere forfeiture, as opposed to waiver, does not extinguish an "error" under
10 Rule 52(b). . . . If a legal rule was violated during the district court
11 proceedings, and if the defendant did not waive the rule, then there has been
12 an "error" within the meaning of Rule 52(b) despite the absence of a timely
13 objection.
14 507 U.S. at 733-34. A forfeited error may be corrected, at the court's discretion. (See Part II.A.3.
15 below.)

19 The district court ruled, alternatively, that Parse waived his right to an impartial jury

26 The district court's notion that "waiver[s]" must be imputed in order to avoid

8 Rule 33 gave the district court discretion to grant a new trial in the interest of justice. 
9 The court's refusal to exercise that discretion on the ground that Parse had waived his right to
10 complain of juror bias was based on errors of law and erroneous findings of fact.
11 3. Plain-Error Review
12 Parse and the amicus council of defense lawyers urge us to adopt a general rule that
13 lawyers need not bring concerns about possible juror misconduct--including concerns about possible
14 misstatements made during voir dire--to the attention of the trial court unless counsel actually know
15 that such misconduct has occurred. We doubt that such a sweeping and absolute rule is appropriate;
16 but in any event, we need not attempt to fashion an appropriate general rule in order to resolve the
17 present appeal. Assuming that the district court was correct in its view that Parse's attorneys should
18 have alerted the court to whatever suspicions they had about Conrad prior to the conclusion of the
19 jury's deliberations, we conclude that the improper presence of Conrad on the jury--a forfeited error--
20 warrants plain-error review and warrants reversal under that standard.
21 Under Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(b), which provides that "[a] plain error that affects
1 substantial rights may be considered even though it was not brought to the court's attention,"
2 before an appellate court can correct an error not raised at trial, there must be
3 (1) "error," (2) that is "plain," and (3) that "affect[s] substantial rights." . . . . 
4 If all three conditions are met, an appellate court may then exercise its
5 discretion to notice a forfeited error, but only if (4) the error "seriously affect[s]
6 the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings."
7 Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. 461, 466-67 (1997) (quoting Olano, 507 U.S. at 732 (other internal
8 quotation marks omitted)). An "error" is an unwaived "[d]eviation from a legal rule." Olano, 507
9 U.S. at 732-33 (internal quotation marks omitted). An error is "plain" if it is "clear" or "obvious." Id.
10 at 734 (internal quotation marks omitted). And an error affects substantial rights when it is
11 prejudicial--that is, when there is a "reasonable probability" that the error affected the outcome of the
12 proceeding. United States v. Dominguez Benitez, 542 U.S. 74, 81-82 (2004) (internal quotation marks
13 omitted). The burden of meeting these criteria for plain-error review, and, if they are met, of
14 persuading the court to exercise its discretion to grant relief based on the fourth factor, is on the
15 defendant. See, e.g., Olano, 507 U.S. at 734-35; Dominguez Benitez, 542 U.S. at 82.
16 We have no difficulty in concluding that Parse meets his burden in the present case. 
17 Here the relevant proceeding was jury selection. The Sixth Amendment gave him the right to be tried
18 before an impartial jury. Conrad, who lied extensively during voir dire, precisely in order to gain a
19 place on the jury, was seated on the jury. As discussed in Parts II.A.1. and I.C. above, such false
20 statements indicate an impermissible partiality, and the district court found that Conrad was actually,
21 impliedly, and inferably biased. The court concluded that, had it known of those falsehoods, it would
22 have dismissed Conrad for cause. It is beyond dispute that there was "error" within the meaning of
23 Rule 52(b) and that the error is "plain" within the meaning of Olano and Johnson.
24 It is also clear on the present record--which includes both the remarkable May Letter
1 that Conrad sent the government after trial and Conrad's statements and hearing testimony thereafter--
2 that the seating of Conrad on the jury affected Parse's substantial rights, as "Conrad was actually
3 biased against Defendants." Daugerdas, 867 F.Supp.2d at 471. In the posttrial investigation, Conrad
4 revealed, inter alia, that she had lied on voir dire believing that the "[d]efendants . . . were 'crooks'." 
5 Id. at 456 (quoting Hr'g Tr. 210, 229). And, with respect to Parse in particular, the court found that
6 in Conrad's May Letter, "[h]er bias . . . bled through when she wrote that she 'did fight the good fight'
7 against acquitting Parse on any counts but eventually had to 'throw in the towel.' Her choice of words
8 shows that Conrad saw herself not as a fact-finder, but as a partisan for 'Our Government.'" Id. at 471
9 (emphases ours).
10 In these circumstances--in which a juror aligned herself with the government, lied
11 pervasively in voir dire for the purpose of avoiding dismissal for cause, believed prior to the
12 presentation of any evidence that the defendants were "'crooks,'" and expressly mentioned Parse as a
13 target of her efforts to persuade the other jurors to convict--a refusal to order a new trial for Parse
14 would seriously affect the fairness, integrity, and public reputation of judicial proceedings.
15 We reverse the district court's denial of Parse's motion for a new trial.
16 B. Parse's Sufficiency Challenges
17 Parse was found guilty of two offenses: mail fraud, in violation of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1341
18 and 2, and corruptly endeavoring to obstruct the administration of the internal revenue laws, in
19 violation of 26 U.S.C. § 7212(a). He argues that he was entitled to a judgment of acquittal on both
20 counts--obviating any new trial--on the ground that the government failed to prove the knowledge or
21 intent elements of those offenses.
1 The mail fraud section makes it a crime to use the mails in furtherance of a "scheme
2 or artifice to defraud, or for obtaining money or property by means of false or fraudulent pretenses,
3 representations, or promises." 18 U.S.C. § 1341. The "essential elements of a mail fraud charge" are
4 "(1) a scheme to defraud, (2) money or property as the object of the scheme, and (3) use of the mails
5 to further the scheme." United States v. Vilar, 729 F.3d 62, 91 n.26 (2d Cir. 2013) (internal quotation
6 marks omitted), cert. denied, 134 S. Ct. 2684 (2014). Because mail fraud is a specific intent crime,
7 the government must "prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was guilty of a conscious
8 knowing intent to defraud," United States v. Regan, 937 F.2d 823, 827 (2d Cir. 1991) (internal
9 quotation marks omitted), cert. denied, 504 U.S. 940 (1992), and "that the defendant contemplated or
10 intended some harm to the property rights of the victim," United States v. Guadagna, 183 F.3d 122,
11 129 (2d Cir. 1999) (internal quotation marks omitted). Federal taxes to which the United States is
12 entitled constitute "property" within the meaning of the mail fraud statute. See, e.g., Fountain v.
13 United States, 357 F.3d 250, 259-60 (2d Cir. 2004), cert. denied, 544 U.S. 1017 (2005).
14 Section 7212(a) makes it unlawful to "corruptly . . . obstruct[] or impede[], or
15 endeavor[] to obstruct or impede, the due administration of" the Internal Revenue Code. 26 U.S.C.
16 § 7212(a). To act or endeavor "corruptly," within the meaning of this section, means to act or
17 endeavor "with the intent to secure an unlawful advantage or benefit either for one's self or for
18 another." United States v. Kelly, 147 F.3d 172, 177 (2d. Cir. 1998) (internal quotation marks
19 omitted).
20 Parse contends that the government failed to establish the specific intent element of
21 § 1341, i.e., his intent to defraud the Internal Revenue Service ("IRS") by depriving it of taxes owed,
22 because the government did not show that he had understood the impropriety of claiming tax losses
1 based on the tax shelter transactions in question (see Parse brief on appeal at 74), and similarly that
2 it failed to establish the "'corruptly' element of §7212(a)" because it did not show that he had "acted
3 with the requisite 'consciousness of unlawfulness'" (Parse brief on appeal at 95). Viewing the
4 evidence in the light most favorable to the government, and crediting every inference that could fairly
5 have been drawn in the government's favor, see, e.g., United States v. Vilar, 729 F.3d at 91; United
6 States v. Biaggi, 853 F.2d 89, 99 (2d Cir. 1988), cert. denied, 489 U.S. 1052 (1989), we reject both
7 of Parse's sufficiency challenges.
8 "Since Gregory v. Helvering, 293 U.S. 465, 55 S.Ct. 266, 79 L.Ed. 596 (1935), it has
9 been settled law that for transactions to be recognized for tax purposes they must have economic
10 substance." Gardner v. Commissioner, 954 F.2d 836, 838 (2d Cir.) (internal quotation marks omitted),
11 cert. denied, 504 U.S. 910 (1992). A transaction lacks economic substance if there was "no reasonable
12 possibility that the transaction would result in a profit," United States v. Coplan, 703 F.3d 46, 92 (2d
13 Cir. 2012) (internal quotation marks omitted), cert. denied, 134 S. Ct. 71 (2013), or if it "can not [sic]
14 with reason be said to have purpose, substance, or utility apart from [its] anticipated tax
15 consequences," Lee v. Commissioner, 155 F.3d 584, 586 (2d Cir. 1998) (internal quotation marks
16 omitted).
17 The trial record here included evidence that Parse was familiar with the economic-
18 substance requirement. He was a certified public accountant, with a master's degree in business
19 administration and years of experience working in financial institutions. He himself used a tax shelter
20 of the type at issue here and received an opinion letter for his personal use, from the law firm that
21 marketed the shelters and employed some of his codefendants. The opinion letter to Parse described
22 the economic substance doctrine in detail; it noted, inter alia, that "a transaction must have economic
1 substance to be respected for federal tax purposes" and that "a transaction which lacks economic
2 substance beyond the creation of tax benefits and which is not imbued with tax-independent
3 considerations is an economic sham without effect for federal income tax purposes." (Government
4 Exhibit 54-1, at M-1 (emphasis added)).
5 The evidence from which a rational juror could infer that Parse knew that transactions
6 he helped to execute for the tax shelters at issue lacked any profit motive included testimony from
7 several witness. One client testified that he attended a tax shelter meeting in which Daugerdas
8 described investments designed to avoid the client's having to pay taxes on his gains on the sale of
9 certain stock. In that meeting, attended by Parse (see Tr. 6242), Daugerdas stated that
10 the profit potential was very low and that going forward, should we choose to
11 go forward, if we were questioned about the matter, that our intent was in fact
12 to make a profit, but in order for this tax shelter to work, there had to be, in
13 effect, a loss to balance off the gains from the stock sale.
14 (Tr. 6244 (emphasis added)). Another client testified that, with respect to a tax shelter transaction
15 involving foreign currency options, Parse "gave me I think a couple" of foreign currencies "to pick
16 from. And it's whether--I mean, it was like, pick either one, it's not going to matter." (Tr. 3850.) 
17 Parse told him there was "a very extreme chance" that the investment could make money, "but
18 generally speaking, it's going to amount to a big loss." (Id.) Another witness described conversations
19 with Parse that he had as a partner in the accounting firm that marketed the tax shelters at issue. Parse
20 explained various aspects of the use of short options transactions, and, as to the potential monetary
21 outcomes, said there was a very minute possibility that a client could "hit a lotto," i.e., make a huge
22 profit on his investment. When the witness had asked whether any client had ever "hit[] the lotto,"
23 Parse responded, in substance, "no, they watch these things pretty closely, and if anybody ever came
1 close, they would move the market." (Tr. 6939-41 (emphasis added).)
2 We conclude that the record was sufficient to permit a rational juror to find that Parse
3 knew that economic substance was essential to the IRS's acceptance of the tax shelter transactions as
4 legitimate, and that he had the specific intent to participate in transactions that had no genuine
5 objective other than depriving the government of taxes.
6 C. Other Contentions
7 Parse also contends that the district court's instructions to the jury with respect to his
8 statute-of-limitations defense to the § 7212(a) obstruction charge were erroneous, and that with respect
9 to both that charge and the mail fraud charge, the instructions were incomplete and deprived him of
10 his defense that he implemented the tax-shelter-related transactions in good faith. We largely
11 disagree.
12 As to the statute-of-limitations defense, the court instructed the jury that "'[i]n order
13 for you to find any defendant guilty [of obstruction], the government must prove that the defendant
14 you are considering or someone involved in the offense committed or caused to be committed an act
15 of obstruction related to the obstruction count on or after' February 3, 2003" (Parse brief on appeal
16 at 101 (quoting Tr. 8907) (emphases in brief)). Parse argues that this was erroneous in part because
17 it allowed the jury to find the prosecution timely as to him on the basis of an act caused or committed
18 by someone else "no matter how remote from the defendant." (Parse brief on appeal at 101.) 
19 However, the language on which Parse focuses was prefaced by the instruction that "[i]f you find that
20 the government has proven all of the elements of the charge of a count beyond a reasonable doubt with
21 respect to a defendant, you must also then consider for certain counts whether the statute of limitations
1 for that count has been satisfied with respect to that defendant" (Tr. 8906 (emphasis added)). Thus,
2 the jury was instructed that it could find the statute of limitations satisfied by an act caused by
3 someone who was "involved in" (Tr. 8907) the offense that the jury, by hypothesis, had already found
4 Parse committed. The government argues that as a whole, the court's instructions told the jury to find
5 the crime was within the limitations period if an act was committed within that period "whether the
6 act was committed by the defendant or someone acting together with the defendant" (government brief
7 on appeal at 61 (emphasis added)), i.e., that that someone "must have been a co-schemer together with
8 the charged defendant being considered" (id.). No doubt the court's instruction would have been
9 clearer had these characterizations been added. We assume that the government, having urged that
10 interpretation upon this Court, should have no objection if such a clarification is requested by Parse
11 and included in the instructions at Parse's retrial.
12 To the extent that Parse also argues that, as a matter of law, the conduct of other
13 persons--such as coconspirators or taxpayers filing returns that calculated their taxes based on losses
14 generated by illegitimate tax shelters--cannot be considered in determining whether the statute of
15 limitations has run on a charge of corruptly obstructing the administration of the tax laws, we leave
16 that question for resolution, if necessary, on the basis of the evidence presented, arguments made, and
17 instructions given at Parse's new trial.
18 As to Parse's other complaints, we conclude that the instructions, read as a whole, see,
19 e.g., Cupp v. Naughten, 414 U.S. 141, 146-47 (1973), were not erroneous. They dealt painstakingly
20 with each element of each category of the offenses charged; the court instructed, inter alia, that "[g]ood
21 faith is a complete defense to each of the charges in the indictment" (Tr. 8905); and the court
22 instructed that, as to each defendant, the government had the burden of proving that he or she willfully
1 committed the crimes charged and did not act in good faith (see Tr. 8897-98, 8905). The trial that was
2 conducted was complex, see, e.g., Daugerdas, 867 F.Supp.2d at 467 ("the largest tax fraud prosecution
3 in U.S. history"); 25 counts against one or more of five defendants were submitted to the jury
4 (requiring the return of 58 verdicts), and the instructions were correspondingly complicated. The court
5 was not required to introduce further intricacies by discussing scenarios for which there was no
6 evidence in the record. The charges against Parse's codefendants have since been resolved; any
7 potential confusion engendered by instructions catering to the complexity of the first trial is unlikely
8 to recur with respect to the two counts at issue in the retrial of Parse alone.
9 CONCLUSION
10 We have considered all of the parties' arguments in support of their respective positions
11 on this appeal, and, except as indicated above, have found them to be either moot or without merit. 
12 The judgment of conviction against Parse is vacated, and the matter is remanded for a new trial in
13 accordance with the foregoing.
