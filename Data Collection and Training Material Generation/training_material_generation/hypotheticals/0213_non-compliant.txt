Source Folder: unknown
Topic: reg_bi_care_expanded
Difficulty: medium
Verdict: NON-COMPLIANT

FACTS
-----
A wirehouse advisor recommends a $185,000 investment in an American Funds Growth Fund (Class A) with a 5.75% front-end load ($10,638 charged immediately) to a 58-year-old client with $420,000 in liquid assets and moderate risk tolerance. The same fund is available in Class F-2 ($24/year flat fee, 0.68% net expense ratio) and Class R-6 (0.28% ER, available through the firm's platform). The advisor documents in CRM notes: 'Client preferred Class A due to commissions relationship.' The advisor receives $5,319 in upfront compensation from Class A; Class F-2 and R-6 generate $0 and $15 annually respectively. No analysis compares share classes or justifies the 5.47% cost differential over a 15-year holding horizon.

QUESTION
--------
Did the advisor comply with Reg BI's care obligation (17 CFR §240.15l-1(a)(2)(i)) when recommending Class A over lower-cost share classes?

ANSWER
------
Non-compliant. Reg BI requires advisors to have a reasonable basis for the recommendation and document their analysis of the interaction between product characteristics and the client's situation. The 5.75% front load plus 0.68% ER versus 0.28% ER (Class R-6) creates a 5.47% cost drag with no documented justification tied to specific client needs. The CRM note explicitly referencing advisor compensation as a factor violates the duty not to put own interests ahead of client interests. While Class A may be appropriate in rare circumstances (e.g., very long holding periods, client preference after disclosure), the bare compensation rationale and absence of comparative analysis breaches care. Similar to FINRA enforcement pattern in share-class cases (e.g., AWC 2019-00018).

APPLICABLE STANDARD
-------------------
Reg BI Care Obligation, 17 CFR §240.15l-1(a)(2)(i); duty to understand product and recommend based on client profile

KEY FACTOR
----------
Advisor failed to document reasonable basis for expensive share class recommendation and CRM notes show compensation—not client benefit—drove the decision.
