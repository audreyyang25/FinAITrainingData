ID: 940
Source Folder: 21_FINRA_OHO_Decisions
Topic: excessive_trading_churning_discretionary_account
Difficulty: medium
Verdict: NON-COMPLIANT

FACTS
-----
Broker Elena Voss managed a discretionary brokerage account for a 58-year-old client with moderate risk tolerance and $280,000 in investable assets. Over an 18-month period, Voss executed 214 trades, generating an annualized cost-to-equity ratio of 31% and a turnover rate of 11.4. The client's account generated $47,000 in commissions for Voss's firm while producing a net loss of $38,000 for the client. Voss did not disclose the cumulative trading costs to the client, and her branch manager approved all trades without reviewing the account's activity metrics.

QUESTION
--------
Does the pattern of trading activity in this discretionary account constitute excessive trading in violation of FINRA rules?

ANSWER
------
No, this conduct is a violation of FINRA Rules 2111 and 2010 (excessive trading/churning).

Courts and FINRA panels apply the cost-to-equity ratio and turnover rate as primary quantitative indicators of churning; a cost-to-equity ratio exceeding 20% and a turnover rate above 6 are generally considered presumptively excessive. Here, both metrics far exceed those thresholds, establishing that trading was conducted to generate commissions rather than to benefit the client's investment objectives. The branch manager's failure to identify and act on these metrics also triggers a supervisory violation under Rule 3110.

APPLICABLE STANDARD
-------------------
FINRA Rule 2111; FINRA Rule 2010; SEC v. Looper & Co. (churning standard); FINRA Rule 3110 (supervision failure)

KEY FACTOR
----------
A cost-to-equity ratio of 31% and turnover rate of 11.4, combined with net client losses and significant commission generation, establish both the quantitative and intent elements of churning.
